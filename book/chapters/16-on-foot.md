# 16. On foot

Leo is across the road, standing under a sign which tells him not to park. He hasn't brought the car. His hands are in his pockets and there is a jacket folded over his arm.

I wait for a bicycle to pass before crossing. The cyclist has a light that flashes straight into my eyes. For several steps the pavement jumps between brightness and dark.

“You could have come up,” I say.

“I could.”

He gives me the jacket. “Try that.”

It is a dark canvas thing with a lining that sticks to my shirt. I put one arm in and he holds the other sleeve while I find it. The cuffs cover my hands. He turns them back once, then twice.

“You're dressed for a much larger woman.”

“It was mine.”

“What happened to the rest of you?”

“Still available.”

I look up at him. He smiles without showing his teeth. It is the first thing anyone has said for an hour that doesn't seem to require an answer from a better person than I am.

He takes the blue bag. I keep it for one extra moment, holding the handle with him, then let him have it.

“Good evening?” he asks.

“Yes.”

“You sound surprised.”

“It was very good. I ate too much.”

“That's generally your test.”

We turn away from the flat. I don't look up at the windows. At the corner I do, but we have already come too far to see them.

“What did you have?”

“Beans on toast.”

“You could have had something better.”

“I wanted beans on toast.”

“With the little saucepan?”

“A different little saucepan. And I watched half a film.”

“Only half?”

“I was enjoying it. I'll finish it.”

There is an off-licence where I remember a shoe shop. Boxes of crisps stand in the window beneath a row of bottles, and behind them a man is cleaning a shelf. He has left one thin stripe of foam along its edge. I watch him go back for it. Leo waits without asking what I am looking at.

“Which way?” he says when I move.

“The river.”

“Long way or short?”

“The way with the bridge.”

“Long, then.”

He changes the bag to his outside hand and offers the other. I slide my hand into it. The jacket cuff gets between our palms; we rearrange ourselves until it doesn't.

The shops end at the old post office. Its steps are still there, but the brass letterbox has been polished shut, with a little plate over the opening. Beyond it the road widens. Cars gather at the lights and pass us in a long breath. We cross on the green man, though there is nothing coming by then.

“I told a story,” I say.

“At dinner?”

“About Jo. The concert. When she made them start again.”

“I remember.”

“She says I promised not to.”

He doesn't answer. We pass beneath a tree and a drop lands on my wrist. I look up, offended. It isn't raining.

“Did I?”

“I wasn't there when you spoke to her.”

“I thought you might remember.”

“I remember you telling me about the song. I don't know what she asked.”

It would be easy for him to say that she was always sensitive, or that I meant well. He might even think one of those things. His hand stays the same weight in mine.

“I made her sound bossy.”

“She was quite bossy.”

“Not that time.”

“Then not that time.”

We reach the bridge. Its lamps light the pavement and the stone parapet, but the water below is mostly a black moving place. Near the far bank a white shape wobbles and lengthens. I think it is something floating, then follow it up to a window in one of the houses.

Leo leans his forearms on the stone. I stand beside him, too short to do the same comfortably. The blue bag rests between his feet.

“I liked being able to tell them something they didn't know.”

“About Jo.”

“About anything. Everybody had come with their own Jo. I had the little one. So I brought her out.”

On the opposite pavement two young men are arguing cheerfully about a bag of chips. One holds it behind his back. The other makes a quick grab and misses. Their voices come across the road, then break up as a car passes.

“She didn't want me to go,” I say.

Leo looks at me.

“Afterwards. She'd thought I'd stay after everyone.”

“Do you want to go back?”

“No.”

The answer comes more quickly here. I am already outside, with the river underneath and his jacket warm at my back. I could go back; the road remains where we have left it. I don't want to.

“All right,” he says.

I put my face against his sleeve. The canvas smells of dust and his kitchen, with something clean underneath. He turns a little so that I can lean without reaching. I am tired of fitting myself to chairs and conversations. For a moment I can fit here.

“Did you miss me?” I ask.

“This evening?”

“No. But thank you for checking.”

He rubs the back of my hand with his thumb. “Yes.”

“Every day?”

He looks down into the water, then at me. “Not in the way you're asking.”

“You don't know how I'm asking.”

“No. I suppose I don't.”

I want to make him answer again. Instead I lift his hand and put my mouth to the knuckle. The skin is cool; the little hairs tickle my lip.

“I missed your hands,” I say, and then have to stop. “That's not right. I haven't been anywhere missing anything.”

“I know.”

“I like them now.”

He opens his fingers. I kiss his palm, which is an awkward public thing to do and therefore quite pleasurable. One of the young men across the road whistles, but I cannot tell whether it is at us or for the chips. I decide it is the chips.

On the far side of the bridge we turn down the slope to the riverside path. There are lamps along it, each with a soft patch of insects around the bulb. Leo swings the bag less as we go down. At the bottom he stops and flexes one leg.

“Knee?”

“It's all right.”

“That wasn't what I asked.”

“Yes. Knee.”

I wait while he bends it again. There is no dramatic wince, just the impatient attention he used to give a stiff drawer.

“Would the short way have been better?”

“The short way has steps.”

“You might have mentioned.”

“I did choose the long way.”

He starts walking. I take the bag from him. This time he releases it immediately.

We pass a bench occupied by a woman eating from a paper tub. A small dog sits upright beside her, staring at each lift of the spoon. The woman doesn't look at us. Her concentration is all on managing the tub, which has begun to fold inward at the rim.

“I want an ice cream,” I say when we have passed.

“You ate too much.”

“That was other food.”

“Nothing open here now.”

“There should be.”

“I'll write.”

“Use your new large handwriting.”

He laughs, then coughs. We stop at another bench, this one empty, and sit. I put the bag beneath my knees, loop a handle round my wrist and stretch my feet in front of me. The shoes have become part of my feet. I cannot remember when I last wore such sensible shoes without despising them.

Leo takes a small bottle of water from his pocket. It is half full. He drinks and passes it over. The rim is warm from his mouth. I drink too much in one swallow and get water down my chin.

“Still available,” I say, giving it back.

He looks at me, then remembers what he said outside the flat. He puts the lid on with elaborate care.

“Yes.”

“Good.”

We sit a little longer. A moth keeps hitting the lamp behind us with a tiny dry sound. I turn to look and cannot see it between the impacts. When it finally flies beyond the light, I feel a foolish satisfaction, as if I helped.

Farther along, the gate by the allotments is locked. Leo puts his hand on it and gives it a pull before looking at the notice.

“That's new.”

“How new?”

“New since Tuesday.”

I look over his shoulder. The opening hours have been written in marker and then crossed out. The new closing time is earlier. Someone has underlined it twice.

“We could get over,” I say.

The gate is barely above my waist. On the other side the path is clear.

“We could,” he says. “I'd prefer not to carry you home afterwards.”

“I could carry you.”

“That's what worries me.”

I put my hand on the top rail. It is cold and damp. I have a brief, strong wish to climb it anyway, not because we need to get through but because I have said I can. Leo waits. He has always been good at waiting in a way that makes me hear myself.

I take my hand away. “Which way, then?”

“Back to the road. Ten minutes more.”

“Ten minutes?”

“Approximately.”

We turn. It is strange to walk back past the same benches when I know how little time I have. The woman and her dog are gone; the tub lies neatly in a bin. I am glad we saw them when they were there. Then I am irritated with myself for making even an ice cream eat its part in my occasion.

At the slope Leo lets me carry the bag and takes my arm. He doesn't hang his weight on it, but uses it on the steepest bit. I shorten my step without announcing it. At the top he keeps hold of me after he no longer needs to.

The road to his cottage runs behind the allotments. Over the fence I can see the tops of sheds, some with little plastic bottles hanging from strings. A light burns in one of them.

“Are they allowed to sleep there?” I ask.

“No idea.”

“You're remarkably uninformed about your neighbourhood.”

“I know where the beans are.”

His gate is painted a dark colour I cannot distinguish under the streetlamp. He lifts it before opening so the latch will clear. Inside, the small garden smells of damp earth. I stand beside him while he finds the key.

“Would you have gone to bed?” I ask. “If I hadn't come.”

“I'd have finished the film.”

“We could finish it.”

He opens the door and turns on the little lamp beside it. “Do you want to?”

I look at his mouth. At the grey stubble above it. There is a tiny cut on his chin that I have somehow not seen all day.

“Not yet.”

He steps back to let me in. I catch his jumper and bring him with me, so that for a moment we are both trying to pass through the doorway at once.
