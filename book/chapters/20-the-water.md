# 20. The water

For the first few feet we go where Leo's push from the landing has sent us. I sit with the oars held still, pleased by a movement I have done nothing to earn.

“Now,” he says.

I lower the blades and pull. One goes into the water. The other makes a long splashing noise on top of it. The boat turns towards the bank.

“Less on that side.”

“Which?”

He points. I look where he is pointing instead of at my hands and let the other blade go too deep. It stops as if caught on something, but when I ease my hand the water releases it. We have travelled perhaps the width of Jo's kitchen, in an uncertain direction.

Leo asks me to stop. I rest the handles as he has shown me. The boat moves quietly without my assistance.

“You needn't get everything at once.”

“That would be a very demanding plan.”

“Just the next stroke.”

I try again. This time both blades enter the water, though not at the same moment. I pull and feel a firm resistance in each hand. The seat presses against the backs of my thighs. Something substantial happens beneath me: the boat moves along its length instead of merely turning about its middle.

“There.”

“I know. I felt it.”

“Good.”

On the next stroke I try to repeat the feeling by pulling harder. It doesn't improve it. Leo holds up one hand and I stop, annoyed with his hand before I am annoyed with myself.

“Small movements,” he says. “You're not dragging furniture.”

I think of the turn in Jo's hall, the top sliding past the radiator, the sharp little sound of the edge against the wall. My hands tighten. I loosen them before I try again.

We go slowly along the bank. Leo watches over my shoulder, sometimes directing one hand more than the other. I am facing where we have come from, which ought to bother me more than it does. The landing becomes smaller behind him. There is no audience on it, only the trolley and the closed gate.

“Don't keep checking the landing,” he says.

“I wasn't.”

He raises his eyebrows.

“I was a little.”

“Look where it's useful.”

“Everything looks useful. I don't know which bit.”

He laughs. “Fair enough.”

He gives me one thing to watch: the angle of the boat against the bank. I can do that. There is a dark branch sticking over the water, then a pale stone, then a patch of reeds. If I work evenly, those things pass along the side instead of coming towards us. I begin to understand how much effort I have spent pointing us at the wrong thing.

The leather we stitched rests in its fitting, moving with the shaft. I want to look at it every time. I settle for seeing it when I lift the blade clear. The sleeve stays where it belongs. That small success is pleasing in a way I could not have guessed while threading the needle.

“Your sewing's holding,” Leo says.

“Did you expect it not to?”

“I'd have replaced it if I did.”

“Without telling me?”

“Probably not.”

“Probably.”

“You were sitting beside it.”

I laugh and lose the rhythm. The right blade hits the surface flat and throws water onto my trouser leg. It is very cold. I say something ugly and Leo laughs harder than the joke requires.

“You could have warned me.”

“About water?”

I lift the dripping oar just enough to suggest a threat. He catches the meaning, puts a hand over his head and says, “No,” with such domestic urgency that I lower it again.

Beyond the reeds a small bird shoots across the water. I see a bright streak and turn too late to find where it lands.

“What was that?”

“Couldn't tell.”

“You must know. You live here.”

“The birds haven't all introduced themselves.”

I had wanted him to name it. A name would have made the sight feel retained. Instead I have a streak of colour which might have been several things. I watch the place it vanished while the boat moves past it.

The air has warmed where the sun reaches across the river. One side of Leo's face is lit and the other remains in shadow. There is a tiny white thread on his orange buoyancy aid. He sees me looking and examines the fastening.

“Something wrong?”

“No.”

“Then row.”

I do. For several strokes there is nothing to correct. The blades catch together; I draw the handles towards me, release them, reach again. My body finds a sequence before I have names for all its parts. I am not graceful, but I am occupied in something that has a result each time.

Leo says nothing. I look at him after the sixth or seventh stroke, wanting him to notice. He is watching the bank. It takes me a moment to recognise that this is the notice I have earned: he no longer has to watch every movement of my hands.

Then one handle catches the other and I make a mess of both sides.

“Ah,” he says.

“Don't sound disappointed.”

“I was remembering.”

“Your first time?”

“Last Thursday.”

We drift while he explains the slight difference in height, one hand passing above the other. I try it without taking another stroke. The boat rocks gently when I lean, and I straighten at once. He tells me I needn't become a statue. I tell him there are limited alternatives available to a beginner.

My palms have begun to feel warm. I lift one from the handle and look at the red line beneath the fingers. The needle left a mark there yesterday. Now the wood has found it.

“Want to rest?”

“Not yet.”

“Then a little more. We turn before that willow.”

He shows me the tree. It is not far away, but distance across water refuses to arrange itself into familiar lengths. I could imagine walking to it in two minutes. Rowing towards it feels like undertaking something larger.

I slow down rather than pull harder. The steadier work brings us closer. On the bank a black dog appears, sniffs a patch of grass and disappears again before its owner comes into view. The owner follows with a lead hanging empty from one hand. He lifts the other to us.

Leo lifts a hand back.

“Do you know him?”

“No.”

“You looked as if you did.”

“You can wave.”

I let go of one handle to wave and the oar begins to move without me. I grab it before it gets far. The man has already turned away. He will never know how much organisation went into my greeting.

We turn short of the willow, as promised. Leo talks me through it in fewer words than I would have used to explain why I couldn't do it. One side works, the other waits. I watch the bank move across his shoulder until we are pointing back towards the landing.

“There,” he says.

“I keep expecting to need more river.”

“You've used quite a lot of this bit.”

The landing is visible again. I have not gone far enough for the shed to disappear behind anything. I like that. We have done something small and it has taken all my attention.

I rest the oars. The wet shafts make dark marks where drops land inside the hull. Leo takes a cloth from beneath his seat and wipes a little pool near his foot.

“Is it leaking?”

“That's yours.”

“I thought it was a useful improvement. Water inside and out.”

He wrings the cloth over the side. I watch his hand, the twist of the wrist and the last drop clinging to the fabric. He puts it back without ceremony.

“When did you decide to build it?”

“I wanted one.”

“I meant what made you think you could.”

“The instructions were very encouraging.”

“Were they right?”

He looks along the boat. “Eventually.”

I wait for more, but that is the amount of explanation he wants to give while sitting inside it. I take another stroke. The handles are becoming familiar in an unreliable way: I know them, then catch one badly and must begin paying attention again.

For a while I don't speak. I can hear little separate sounds: the leather moving, water leaving the blades, the faint knock of something in Leo's pocket against the seat. A car passes somewhere behind the sheds. Its engine grows louder, then is gone. From this low position the bank looks full of places I couldn't see from the path. Roots, a small dark tunnel in the soil, a bottle trapped beneath a branch. I want to pick up the bottle and cannot reach it without doing something foolish, so it stays where it is.

My back begins to tire. I don't announce it, but Leo sees the shortening of the stroke.

“We can go in.”

“What time is it?”

He looks. “Twenty to six. A bit after.”

There is enough time for another turn, perhaps. I look towards the willow. Then I look at the landing, which has moved closer while I have been deciding.

“I'd rather get us back while I can still do it.”

“Good.”

I want to tell him not to say good in that voice, but this time the voice is his and the annoyance is mine. I keep rowing.

Close to the landing he gives me smaller directions. We need to approach at an angle that makes sense to him and not yet to me. I follow what he asks, sometimes a stroke late. He tells me to let the boat move instead of attempting to correct everything at once.

We come alongside too far from the boards. He doesn't stretch or leap. He has me work one oar lightly, then stop, then try again. The second approach is better. He reaches the painter and passes it around a cleat, holding us close while I bring the blades in.

“Stay seated a moment.”

I obey. My legs have forgotten they exist and are pleased to be left alone. Leo gets out first, using the landing rather than my shoulder. He holds the boat while I rise.

On the bank the dog walker has returned by the road. His dog stops to examine the trolley. Somewhere behind him, beyond the gate, a person in a dark coat lifts a flask to avoid catching it on the latch.

Jo.

She has not seen that I have seen her. She is concentrating on the gate, whose catch she knows but cannot manage with both hands occupied. I stand too quickly. The boat moves and Leo says my name, sharply.

I sit down again. Jo looks towards us at the sound. She lifts the flask once, a small greeting, and puts it on the ground so she can open the gate properly.

“In a minute,” I call, though she hasn't asked me to hurry.

Leo offers his hand. This time I take it, put one foot where he shows me and come up onto the landing slowly, with the boat still moving beneath the other foot until it lets me go.
