# 14. The story I tell

Nina finds the pencils before anyone has agreed to play anything.

“Paper,” she says. “We need paper.”

Jo gets up and takes a few sheets from the pile beneath the window. She checks the backs before giving them to Nina. There are pale grey lines across one sheet, the remains of something printed badly enough to be useless. Nina folds it into quarters and tears along the folds.

“Drawing,” she says.

“No portraits,” Patrick says.

“Objects. You write an object, put it in the bowl, then someone draws it.”

“Someone else,” Jo says.

“Yes. Obviously someone else.”

Phil looks at the loaf, or what remains of it. “How recognisable does it have to be?”

“That may be a question for your partner.”

We find places for the plates, pushing them towards the sides of the tables. Jo takes the bowl with the pencils and receipt, empties it carefully and wipes out a trace of soil from Nina's plant. The receipt goes on the shelf beside the folded gas bill. I recognise the little parcel holding the piece I broke off this morning. She has room for both the thing she bought and the damage we brought home with it.

I write FISH SLICE on my scrap and fold it in two. It seems straightforward until I remember how many sorts of object a person might imagine from those two words. I almost change it, then put it in the bowl. Let somebody else deal with my contribution.

Mara is my partner. We turn our chairs slightly towards one another, and she moves her glass away from my elbow before I begin. I take out a slip which says UMBRELLA. The pencil point breaks as I make the curved top. Jo finds a sharpener, emptying its little container over the kitchen bin before handing it to me. The care with which she does this feels excessive until I realise whose floor it is.

I draw the umbrella. Mara says mushroom, lamp, jellyfish. I put rain around it.

“Leaking jellyfish.”

“Umbrella,” Patrick says from the other table.

“You're not on our side,” Nina tells him.

“I wasn't aware we had sides.”

“We've had several conversations about it.”

Mara draws a washing machine which I think is a safe. She adds a sock. I say a safe for socks, and Ellen laughs so hard that she spills water onto her napkin. Mara circles the sock with the pencil, looking at me with an impatience so frank that I like her immediately.

“Washing machine.”

She drops the pencil in triumph. We claim the point though nobody has remembered to start a score.

The drawings pass round. Arun produces a ladder which Ellen takes for a railway, then for an unusually thin church. He gets it back, adds a person halfway up and holds it towards her. She says window cleaner, which he accepts until Jo points out that the object on the slip is still a ladder.

“You can't award yourself sympathy points,” Jo says.

“Watch me,” Ellen tells her.

I am laughing enough to feel it in my stomach. The button I loosened has been fastened again, and I regret the tidiness. There is cake left on my plate. I eat a little icing from the fork while Mara and Phil argue about whether a kettle needs a handle in order to be a kettle.

Jo has come back to her chair. She sits with one foot beneath her, an old position in a body too large to make it look quite the same. She gives Patrick a clean sheet and tells him not to show the word to Nina. He says he is drawing with his other hand to make it fair, and Nina asks whom he is being fair to.

I could stay here for hours. The thought surprises me because I have spent so much of the day counting them. There is something I have wanted from Jo which is available only when she is doing something else: the pleasure of being near her without having to make her look back.

Then my fish slice comes out of the bowl.

Phil draws a fish, an accomplished fish with scales, and cuts it in half with a line. Ellen says fish finger. Nina says fish pie. Arun says surgery. I know the answer and cannot give it without admitting that I wrote it. I laugh before anyone else does, which makes Jo look at me.

“You put that in.”

“I can't be the only person who knows a fish slice.”

“No,” Arun says. “But you appear to be the only person who wants one pictured.”

I draw the kitchen tool myself, badly enough that Mara says fly swatter. By the time we get there the paper is covered with additions and Ellen has folded her wet napkin into a shape which will hold no more water.

Jo collects the scraps so that Patrick cannot put the same word back into the bowl. She catches him doing it and taps his hand with the pencil.

“Once each.”

“I'm trying to improve the standard.”

“You can write a new one.”

Nina leans towards me. “Was she always this firm?”

It is an invitation so neat that I do not have to invent it for myself. Jo is looking at Patrick's new slip, trying to read through the paper.

“She once stopped an entire concert,” I say.

Jo looks up.

“Oh, no.”

There is a smile at the corner of her mouth. I choose to take the smile rather than the words. I already have the room: Nina leaning forward, Phil putting his pencil down, Ellen looking across at me with something in her expression I do not stop to inspect.

“She was ten,” I say. “School concert. They'd put her in this white blouse with a collar so large you could have landed a small aircraft on it.”

“The blouse was mine,” Ellen says.

“Then you should have charged for air traffic.”

She laughs. I can feel the story find its pace. I describe the hall with its high windows, the chairs which had been unfolded from somewhere dusty, the headteacher walking up the middle as if he were entering a cathedral. Jo stood at the front with her hands down her sides. Her hair had been brushed until it shone.

“It had a great dent from the elastic,” Jo says.

“That too. A very polished dent.”

The laugh arrives where I expect it. I have missed this, I realise: being able to put a small thing in front of other people and have them make it larger with me.

“The piano starts,” I say. “She opens her mouth, makes about half a note, and then puts her hand up. Not a timid hand. The hand you use to stop a bus.”

I hold mine up. Mara smiles. Patrick leans back to see me properly from the folding table.

“Then she says, ‘From the beginning.’”

I do the voice, a little higher than Jo's was even then, the chin lifted. There is a way of holding her mouth I remember perfectly. Or perfectly enough for them to recognise something they know.

“They start again?” Phil asks.

“Oh, they have to. She's planted her feet. She gives this stamp, just once, as if to establish who is in charge. The headteacher is hovering. The pianist has both hands suspended above the keys. Nobody in the hall dares move. Ten years old, and she's conducting the adults.”

Nina laughs. Arun laughs too, a quick surprised sound. I am pleased with how little I have needed to add to make them see it. Jo's fingers are gathering the empty paper slips into a small stack.

“It was the wrong key,” she says.

I look at her.

“Yes. Exactly. She knew what she wanted.”

“No. It was too high. He started it too high.”

The stack slides beneath her thumb. She squares it against the table and puts the pencil on top.

“I'd already asked Mrs Vale to make sure. We'd practised it lower.”

“I thought that was the admirable part,” I say. “You wouldn't be pushed about.”

“I didn't stamp my foot.”

I can see a foot stamping. I can see the polished floor, the white sock wrinkling above a black shoe. The image is very clear and gives me no help at all in deciding whether it happened.

“Perhaps that was another occasion.”

“It wasn't that one.”

“Then I've added it.”

I mean this lightly. Acknowledging the addition ought to make it harmless, to give her back the real event while allowing us to keep the enjoyable version. She does not smile.

Mara picks up her glass. Patrick looks towards the kitchen, where something has clicked as it cools. Ellen begins folding the wet part of her napkin inwards again, though there is no dry part left.

“What did you sing?” Nina asks Jo.

“A song Mrs Vale chose.”

“Did it go all right after?”

“Yes.”

Jo looks at her and manages a smile. “I could reach the notes. That helped.”

Nina nods. She is not withdrawing the laugh she gave me. It belongs to something which was funny when I said it. I cannot get it back from her any more easily than I can take back the lifted chin.

The memory comes then, not the hall but the car beforehand. Jo beside me, testing a note under her breath. Her hand flattening the edge of the blouse where it lay over the seat belt. She was afraid to ask for the lower key again. She said Mrs Vale would think she was making a fuss, that she had already asked once and ought to manage.

I had told her to ask. Of course I had. I had said there was nothing wrong with needing the song where she could sing it.

And afterwards, when I had begun to describe her to Leo, standing so squarely at the front of the hall, she had caught my sleeve.

Don't make it one of those stories.

I had understood exactly what she meant. I had promised. There had been other things to tell Leo, and I had told them instead. The promise had been small enough to keep without congratulating myself. Now I have lost it in front of seven people because I wanted to hear them laugh.

Arun takes a slip from the bowl. “I've got something impossible.”

“Show Ellen,” Phil says. “She'll call it a railway.”

Ellen takes the pencil. She draws an oval, then an uncertain line, and the room begins to move again. Jo gets up with two plates. I reach for one, but she shifts both away from my hand.

“I've got them.”

She goes into the kitchen. I can hear the water running, though two plates would not have needed it for so long.

I look at the people still sitting here. I could follow her and apologise in private, leaving the story as I told it among them. That would be easier. It might even look considerate.

“There wasn't a stamp,” I say.

Ellen stops drawing.

“And she'd told the teacher about the key before the concert. She wasn't trying to run it. She was trying to sing the part they'd practised.”

“I'd have asked them to stop too,” Nina says.

“Yes.”

I do not add how admirable I find that. I have already made my admiration perform several jobs it is not suited for.

From the kitchen Jo puts a plate into the draining rack. I do not know how much she heard. She comes back with water and fills the glasses which are low, starting at the far end of the table. When she reaches mine she pauses to move the fork away from its base.

“Thank you,” I say.

She nods, then pours.

Ellen's drawing turns out to be a corkscrew. The oval was meant to be a hand. Phil says the hand has made matters substantially worse, and Ellen gives him the pencil to demonstrate improvement. He draws something which makes Patrick laugh into his glass. Jo laughs too, briefly, looking over Nina's shoulder.

The fact that she can still enjoy herself ought to be a comfort. I find myself wanting her to look back at me so I can be certain it includes me. I pick up my fork instead and finish the cake I have been neglecting. The icing has begun to dry at its cut edge.

Mara leans across to ask whether I want another turn. I do. I nearly say no because I have behaved badly, as if refusing myself a game would settle an account with Jo. Instead I take a clean sheet and let Mara choose a word. She writes it with her hand covering the paper and gives it to me face down.

BICYCLE.

I draw two wheels. They are different sizes. I join them with a frame which will not support anyone, add handlebars at the wrong end, cross them out and put them elsewhere. Mara watches, frowning.

“Bicycle.”

“Yes.”

“I thought I'd better say it before you added a third wheel.”

I laugh. It is a smaller laugh, but it is still mine. Jo is answering something Patrick has asked about the table. She has one hand on its edge, beside the pale chip. She is showing him how the legs come off, with no intention of taking them off now.

I look at the drawing on the paper. There is nothing of my daughter in it. Mara turns it round, tries to work out which way it would travel, and puts it with the others.

Near the window the light has thinned. Somebody closes it because the room has begun to cool, and Ellen finds a space on the sideboard for the remaining cake. The candles are back in their little box. Jo checks the time, then collects the last dinner plates from the smaller table. I stand to help, and this time she gives me two. I carry them into the kitchen, careful to keep the cutlery from sliding, while behind us Nina unfolds another blank sheet.
