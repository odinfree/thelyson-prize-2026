# 13. Dinner

The rice has stuck.

Jo discovers this after she has put the aubergines down, when we are already reaching for things. She lifts the lid and looks into the saucepan with an expression which makes Arun stand before she says anything.

“Leave the bottom,” he says.

“I know.”

She tries to loosen it with the spoon. A dark edge comes up with the white rice and she stops. I can smell it now, a dry, hot smell beneath the tomato and lemon.

“I turned it down.”

“It was on for a while.”

“I know that as well.”

Arun takes an empty bowl from the counter. He does not take the saucepan from her. He holds the bowl while she lifts out what will come away easily, leaving the scorched layer undisturbed. There is less than she intended. She looks at the eight plates and back at the bowl.

“We've got bread,” Phil says.

“Quite a lot of bread,” Nina adds.

“And lentils,” says Arun.

“I know we've got other food.” Jo puts the saucepan in the sink. “I wanted this food.”

Nobody tells her it doesn't matter. It does not matter very much, but she is entitled to have wanted her rice. I take the bread knife, put Phil's loaf on the board and begin to cut it.

The crust resists. I turn it on its side and saw through from the softer end, making slices thinner than the ones I cut at lunch. Phil watches the first come away intact and reaches for it, then draws back because I still have the knife.

“That's encouraging.”

“Did you think it was hollow?”

“I wasn't certain what I'd made.”

I hand him the slice. He bends it and smells it, looking rather like Jo with the table. Then he breaks it in two and gives half to Mara. She eats it while he waits, anxious and trying to look occupied with his glass.

“Bread,” she says.

“Thank God.”

By the time I have filled a basket, Jo has brought out the usable rice. The darker grains have been left behind. She puts the bowl within reach of the smaller table and sits down. Arun passes her the salad before helping himself.

We eat for a minute with the concentration that follows a meal taking longer to arrive than expected. Knives touch plates. The borrowed oval dish travels only as far as Jo and me because it is too heavy to pass comfortably; I serve the people beyond us, and Arun brings the two plates from the folding table. Patrick has a substantial portion for a man who has eaten. Nina notices but leaves him alone.

The aubergine is soft inside and firmer at the edges, the tomato sharp enough to make the dressing useful. I put a spoonful of my thicker portion against it. Jo watches the movement and then looks away before I taste it.

“It's good,” I say.

She nods, mouth full. She does not need to make a ceremony of believing me.

I eat another piece. I would have used more garlic. I am glad I have not, because I can taste the smoky part of the sauce underneath. There is no way to explain this without seeming to offer a verdict, so I take more sauce and let her notice if she wants.

At the other table Arun's knee catches a chair leg. Patrick moves his foot and the chair tilts slightly, then settles. They adjust themselves in opposite directions and carry on eating. The arrangement looks less elegant than the one I imagined for Jo while I was looking at the flowers, and more convincing.

Nina asks about the table. Jo tells the story of getting it round the turn, giving Arun the difficult end and herself credit for the measurements. When she reaches my part, she says, “Mum saved the doorframe,” and touches the pale break in the edge.

“I also did that,” I say.

“Yes. We managed both.”

Patrick leans across to look. “You could put a bit—”

“Not tonight,” Jo says.

He nods. “Fair enough.”

I feel a warm rush towards her which includes relief at not having to hear another solution. The chip remains where it is, visible beside my knife.

Phil tells us about trying to carry a mattress upstairs alone. He had taken off the packaging too soon, and once it expanded it blocked the landing. He spent most of the evening on the wrong side of it. Ellen asks why he did not ring someone, and he says he could not bear the number of people who would have been pleased to help. I laugh hard enough to put down my fork.

“You understand,” he says to me.

“I understand wanting to report success afterwards.”

“Yes. Exactly.”

Mara says the mattress is still too big for the room. Phil says that was a separate miscalculation and should not be added to the first. They have been in each other's houses. I like hearing it without having to be told what they are to one another.

Jo asks Mara about a meeting, and the conversation changes into something which has apparently been troubling them both for several days. People in an office have been told to book desks which are already occupied by people who have not booked them. Patrick knows a similar arrangement somewhere else and offers an elaborate explanation of why it exists. Nina interrupts him before he reaches the second explanation.

“We don't need to admire the system.”

“I wasn't admiring it.”

“You were beginning to understand it at length.”

Jo laughs with her head bent over her plate. I watch her laugh while everyone else watches Patrick. It is not the same laugh as the one in the warehouse, close against my ear. This one is less guarded, almost untidy. She makes a small sound at the end when she has run out of breath.

I want to tell them she has always done that. For once I manage to let her do it without giving its history.

Ellen passes me the water. Our hands knock against each other at the handle. She mouths sorry and I shake my head. Across the table Jo is explaining that on her latest job nobody could agree what the wall was supposed to look like.

“The shop by the old bus stop?” Mara asks.

“Yes. The one with the terrible green front.”

“I like the green.”

“You can buy the leftover paint.”

“What was wrong with the wall?” I ask.

“Nothing, depending on which wall you wanted.”

She tells us about taking down shelves in a building being changed into a café. Under the shelves, beneath several layers of lining paper, they found part of an old painted sign. Red letters on cream plaster. The top of a word, a price, the edge of a blue border. It had not survived as a complete picture. It had survived in the places people could not easily get at.

“The owner wanted to keep it,” Jo says. “At first.”

“Until the price,” Patrick says.

“Until he saw how little of it there was. He thought it would say something. It says part of SOAP, and a number nobody agrees on.”

Phil suggests washing, which gives the rest of us time to tell him that we understood. Jo draws the shape of a letter with a finger above the table, stopping short of the wet ring beside her glass.

“There's a newer layer over it in another place,” she says. “A yellow stripe. Somebody painted straight across the lettering. That's part of it too.”

“Would you keep the stripe?” I ask.

“I'd record it.”

“But would you keep it?”

She considers this. “I don't get to decide alone. I measure what's there, make the record, say what we found. Other people have to work out what can stay on the wall.”

I had liked the idea of her uncovering things, bringing them back. Her actual work contains more paper and fewer satisfying endings than the version I was preparing to admire.

“So what will happen?”

“They may leave a small section showing. Or cover it in a way that doesn't destroy it. They're still arguing about where the coffee machine goes.”

“The coffee machine has a vote,” Arun says.

“It has most of the votes.”

We laugh. Jo takes another piece of bread and wipes sauce from her plate.

“The awkward thing is the owner wants to have the missing letters painted in,” she says. “So people can read it.”

“Would that be so bad?” Ellen asks.

“Not if everyone knew which bits were new. But he wants them to look the same.”

“I'd want to read it,” I say.

“So would I.”

“Then you could make a good guess.”

“We could make several.”

She says this gently enough that I hear my own insistence.

“What colour was the blue?” I ask.

She looks at me. “Quite dark where it was covered. Much paler at the edge.”

“Could you show me?”

She gets out her phone. There is a picture of the wall, close enough that I can see torn paper and the coarse surface of the plaster. Her finger rests beside a line of blue. I lean towards it. For a moment the room grows quieter around us, though nobody has stopped talking.

“There,” she says. “That's the bit I like.”

I can see why. The border turns a corner with a small unevenness where the painter changed direction. It is not important enough to become the surviving part of a sign. It is only where a hand hesitated.

Jo puts the phone away when Nina asks for the dressing. I pass it, glad the picture is not mine to keep passing round.

We have enough food. This becomes apparent slowly, as the urgency leaves the serving. Patrick takes the last aubergine half and divides it with Nina. Arun returns the nearly empty rice bowl to the kitchen. Ellen scrapes lentils from the side of the tub with the determination of somebody who has washed enough containers to know what will otherwise be wasted.

I am full in a way I have not been since before I was ill. The pressure is comfortable and inconvenient. I loosen the top button of my trousers beneath the table, then look up to find Mara doing something similar under her napkin. We exchange a glance and neither says anything.

Jo gets up for the cake. Ellen goes with her, pretending to be needed for the matches. The rest of us move glasses and plates to make a space. Phil collects the bread basket and Patrick folds napkins into a heap that Nina immediately separates from the clean ones.

We hear Ellen say, “Not that knife,” and Jo answer, “It's a cake, not a cable.” Then they come back together.

The candles are small and leaning. Their flames move towards me as Jo lowers the cake, then stand upright when her hands become still. We sing. Jo looks at each of us in turn, smiling with her lips pressed together as if she is holding in an answer. When she reaches me, I am still singing, louder than I intended.

She blows the candles out in two breaths. Ellen catches one before the wax reaches the icing. Somebody claps and then we all clap, a brief ridiculous noise for eight people in a small room.

“Thirty,” Jo says, looking at the smoke.

“It suits you,” Mara says.

“You haven't seen enough of it yet.”

Jo pulls out the first candle and wipes the icing off with a finger. She eats it. I had forgotten that she would do that in front of people. Or perhaps she would not have, and has learned.

She cuts the cake into more slices than there are plates, keeping some for tomorrow. A wedge lands on my plate with its point folded under. She tries to straighten it, but I stop her with my fork.

“I'll manage.”

She leaves it. The lemon is sharp, the icing too sweet by itself and right with the cake. Ellen watches me take the first mouthful. I give her the small nod she has been waiting for.

Outside the open window, a car goes past with music coming from it. For a moment we all hear somebody else's evening. Then Jo asks Phil for the knife, Nina reaches for her glass, and I take another mouthful before the taste of the first has gone.
