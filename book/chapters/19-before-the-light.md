# 19. Before the light

The sound enters a dream I cannot keep. For a moment it belongs to a machine beside a road, then Leo reaches past me and it is a phone on the bedside table.

“Sorry,” he says.

I have woken on his side. My hand is underneath my cheek and quite numb. I sit up too quickly. The room tips a little, settles, becomes a room again.

“What time?”

“Ten to four.”

“That's ridiculous.”

“Your appointment.”

He switches the alarm off. The dark returns, complete except for the edge of the curtains. I could put my head down. I could make him say we needn't go. My body has become heavy in exactly the shape the bed can hold.

He doesn't say it. He sits on the edge with his feet on the floor, rubbing a hand over his face. His back is a broad pale shape in the dark. I put my hand against it and feel the warmth through his T-shirt.

“Are you awake?” I ask.

“In stages.”

He finds the lamp. We dress without talking. I put my socks on properly this time and find a hair caught inside one of them. It is grey and short. I pick it off and leave it on the sheet, then think this is unkind to whoever changes the bed and pick it up again. There is nowhere sensible to put it. I let it fall.

In the bathroom the mirror is misted at the lower edge from yesterday's washing. My face looks puffy. The little bruise on my forehead from the boat-shed door has found its colour overnight. I touch it and make it hurt more.

There is a spare toothbrush still in its packet on the shelf. Leo left it there for me yesterday. The sight of it, waiting through all the things we did instead, makes me grip the edge of the basin. I am frightened of the morning now in a different way: less like a thought than a bad step at the top of the stairs. My stomach lifts and doesn't come down.

“Flora?”

“Coming.”

I open the packet. The plastic resists until I use my teeth, which is an idiotic way to begin cleaning them. I brush carefully, watching the mirror without meeting my own eyes. When I spit, there is a little pink in the foam. I have pressed too hard.

In the kitchen Leo has put the kettle on. He is standing at the back door, looking into the garden. I join him. The air is cold against my wet mouth.

“Nothing much moving,” he says.

I look for what he means. A leaf trembles on a plant by the wall. Farther off I hear a bird give one brief experimental phrase, then stop. The sky over the fence is not quite the same dark as the fence itself.

“Does that mean we can go?”

“We'll look when we get there.”

He closes the door and takes his phone from the table. He checks the forecast and the river information he uses, frowning more at the screen than at what it says. I do not ask for a cheerful version. After a while he puts the phone down.

“Looks all right. No reason to decide against it here.”

“Good.”

“Toast?”

“Something more than toast.”

He considers the cupboard, then opens the fridge. “Egg?”

“An ordinary one.”

He gives me a look and puts two eggs in a small pan. I make the toast. It comes out pale on one side and dark on the other. I turn the slices and put them in again before he can object.

“I usually live with that,” he says.

“I haven't time to get used to it.”

The words land between us. Neither of us rescues them. He turns the heat under the eggs down and I stand beside the toaster, willing the second side to catch up without burning the first.

We eat at the kitchen table. The eggs are softer than he meant them to be. He apologises and I tell him they are exactly right, which is untrue. I prefer a firmer yolk. I eat it anyway, lifting the cup to keep a drip off the table.

“Jo called,” I say.

“You told me.”

“She may come. At six.”

“I thought she might.”

“Don't sound too certain.”

He looks up from his plate. “I said might.”

“Yes. You did.”

I have begun arguing with a version of him that hasn't spoken. He peels the last piece of shell off his egg and puts it beside the others. There is a small piece still on the white. I point to it. He finds it, puts it down and goes on eating.

At twenty past four the window begins to contain things again. The nearest plant, the rim of a pot, the line of his shed roof. I take my cup to it and stand with the warm china against my chest.

“Your tomatoes would go there?”

He comes to see where I mean. “That gets shade most of the morning.”

“That's why you have only one tomato.”

“They weren't there.”

“Oh.”

He points to the other side of the little yard. I can just see three empty pots. The sticks are still in them, leaning together. The sign is gone.

I am glad he has not cleared the sticks away. I cannot say why without making them carry more than sticks should have to carry. I drink my tea and let him explain where he will put the new plants.

We wash what we have used. I dry the pan with the corner of a towel that he tells me is meant for hands, and apologise, and he says it doesn't matter now. The ordinary friction comforts me until I become aware of taking comfort from it. Then it is only a pan again, and I put it away.

He lays out what we need by the door: his keys, the shed key, a torch, the phone, the blue bag and the canvas jacket. The bag has been in the kitchen all night. I check the inner zip by touch. The envelope is there, stiff against the lining.

“Shall we walk?” I ask.

“We'll take the car. Easier with everything afterwards.”

Afterwards. He is able to use the word to mean a time in which bags and coats need transporting. I am grateful for that use of it.

At the door I stop. Not because I have forgotten anything. I put my hand on the frame and cannot make the next movement. Behind me are the unmade bed, the cups still warm in the cupboard, the pale mark where I have wiped the table. Leaving this house feels like agreeing to something I have until now been able to keep discussing.

Leo waits on the path. He has already locked the car once by mistake while trying to open it. The bag hangs from his hand.

“You all right?”

“No.”

He puts the bag down. “We needn't go.”

I shake my head, which doesn't explain anything.

“I want to go. I don't want to leave.”

He comes back to the step. He doesn't take my hand off the frame. “We can have another minute.”

I look past him at the car. Its windscreen is wet. A bird lands on the gate, tilts forward and flies away before I can decide what it is.

“Don't count it,” I say.

“I wasn't going to.”

We stand there. He rubs his jaw, looks into the kitchen as if he too might have forgotten something. I let go of the frame. My palm leaves a faint damp print on the paint.

I close the door myself and he locks it. Then we get into the car and he waits for the windscreen to clear, another ordinary delay for which I am unexpectedly thankful.

The roads are almost empty. We pass a van outside a bakery, its rear doors open. I smell warm bread through the vent and am hungry again, though I have just eaten. A man in a white coat lifts two trays together. Leo slows to give him space when he steps into the road.

“We could have stopped,” I say after we pass.

“You want breakfast twice?”

“I am very much alive to the possibility.”

He laughs, and I am pleased with the joke because I didn't prepare it. It arrived and he laughed and we drove on.

At the shed he parks on the hard patch by the wide gate. The sky has become a pale grey above the roofs. The river is still in the shade, but I can see its far edge and a line of small ripples moving past a moored boat. Nothing in it resembles the black water beneath the bridge last night.

We leave the blue bag in the locked car. I put the jacket on, cuffs rolled back, and follow him to the shed. This time I bend before I reach the little door.

“Learned something,” I say.

“Don't become smug. There's the way out still.”

The shed smells colder than it did yesterday. He turns on a light and puts his keys on a hook. The boat is where we left it. The repaired oars rest beside it, their leather sleeves dry and dark. I run a finger over my stitches and find the one that lies crooked.

Leo examines both sleeves, the fittings on the boat and the inside of the hull. I hold the torch where he asks, although the shed light is on, and understand how useful it is to have one small bright place exactly where you need it. He doesn't hurry because I am watching.

“Anything wrong?”

“No. Looking.”

He gives me a buoyancy aid and helps me adjust it. I pull the fastening too tight, then loosen it until he tells me to stop. He checks that it will stay in place. His own is orange, the one in the photograph. I like seeing him put it on for something which has not happened yet.

We take the boat down on its trolley. I am at the bow, steering, while he controls the other end. The wheel hits a stone and stops so abruptly that I nearly keep walking without it. We back up, move the stone and continue. By the water there is enough light to see the little green weeds between the boards of the landing.

“Wait here a moment,” he says.

We stand with the boat still on the trolley. He watches the water, the narrow channel beyond the moorings and the branches on the other bank. I follow his gaze without pretending I know everything he is assessing. A duck paddles out from beneath the landing and shakes itself. Drops shine briefly on its back.

The sun reaches the upper windows across the river. I look at Leo's watch when he turns his wrist: a little after five-ten. He isn't delaying to use up time. He is waiting until the outing we have chosen can be the outing he knows how to make.

“We stay this side,” he says at last. “A little way along, then back. If I ask to take over, you let me.”

“Yes.”

“Even if you've nearly got it.”

“That sounded personal.”

“It was.”

I help him lower the boat. Water rises along the blue paint, stopping where the boat's weight has put it. He holds it against the landing while I step down where he shows me. This is quite different from stepping into it on the shed floor. I wait for the small movement beneath my feet to finish. It doesn't finish; it becomes the thing we are doing.

I sit. He puts the oars within reach and gets in after me, moving slowly enough for me to see what his weight does. The boat settles again.

On the bank the empty trolley leans against the fence. Leo reaches back, releases the painter and coils it inside. It is nearly half past five. I put my hands where he showed me, one on each handle, and wait for him to tell me which way to push.
