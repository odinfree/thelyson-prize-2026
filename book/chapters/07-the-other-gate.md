# 7. The other gate

In the car I open the window. The air coming in smells of warmed rubber and the bakery at the corner. Leo asks whether I am cold, and I say no. He leaves the window as it is.

For a little while we do not speak. I have been looking at Jo or listening for her for so much of the morning that the space beside me feels unusually wide. The seat is soft under my thighs. I let my head rest against it and watch the shop fronts pass.

“She's doing well,” I say.

“Yes.”

“You could sound more pleased.”

“I am pleased.”

“I know.”

He turns right at the lights. I lift my head. This is a way I know. The little traffic island has been widened, and there is a bollard where there used to be a bent sign, but the road rises at the same gentle angle. At its far end a row of houses faces the school wall. Behind the wall are trees which hide most of the roof.

“Are we going home first?”

He slows, though there is nothing in front of us.

“We're going to mine.”

“That's what I said.”

He keeps both hands on the wheel. There is a new patch on the road, dark across the paler surface. The tyres make a different sound as we pass over it.

“I don't live there now.”

I look at him. He is watching the road too carefully.

“Where?”

“At the house. I sold it.”

We pass the school entrance. The metal gates are closed. A yellow plastic thing, perhaps a toy shovel, is lying inside them.

“You sold our house.”

“Yes.”

“When?”

“After Jo had moved out. A few years after.”

I look ahead. In a moment there will be the turn. I know the shops before it, the little wall, the place where you can see over the roofs towards the river. Leo puts on his indicator.

“You're going past it.”

“The road goes through. To where I am now.”

“Stop.”

“There isn't anywhere just—”

“Then where there is.”

He turns. The houses come towards me, one pair after another. Some have new windows. The red garage door on the corner is grey. I have time to notice all of this before our house arrives, as though the street is laying out evidence that I ought to have been prepared.

The hedge has gone.

That is the first thing I see. The thick hedge which used to scrape against the gate is gone, and in its place is a low fence with widely spaced uprights. Through it I can see almost the entire front garden. There are paving stones, two large pots and a little tree with dark red leaves. A woman is kneeling beside one of the pots. Her bottom is towards us.

Leo parks on the opposite side, further down. He switches off the engine but does not take out the key.

“Flora.”

“I can see it.”

“I know.”

The woman gets up. She puts both hands against her hips and bends backwards, looking at the sky. She is wearing a checked shirt and old trainers. A smear of soil runs from her wrist to her elbow.

“You didn't tell me.”

“I was going to.”

“When we reached the other house?”

He looks down at the wheel. I wait, but he doesn't make an excuse. It is worse, in a way. There is nothing for me to argue with except what has already happened.

“I knew this would hurt,” he says.

“So you thought you'd make it a surprise.”

“No.”

“It is one.”

“Yes.”

I open the door. He reaches towards me, then lowers his hand before it touches my arm.

“I'll come with you.”

“I'm only looking.”

He gets out anyway. We cross when the road is clear, and I go to the gate. It is a different gate too, lighter, with a loop of wire beside the latch. I put my hand on it without knowing what I mean to do.

The woman looks up from the pot.

“Hello.”

“Hello.”

I have no opening after that. Behind her the front door is blue. We painted it red in a summer when Jo was small enough to keep touching it before it dried. The door itself is the same, with the oval patch around the handle where the paint has worn differently. I stare at the patch.

“Can I help?”

“I used to live here.”

Her eyes move to Leo. “Oh. Yes. Leo, isn't it?”

“Hello, Hazel.”

“I thought I recognised you.”

She brushes her hands on her trousers, making the soil spread rather than disappear. I feel caught between them, the one person who should know the place and does not know how to introduce herself at its gate.

“I'm Flora.”

Hazel looks at me again. Her face changes slowly enough that I see each part of it. Her mouth opens a little, then closes. She glances towards the house, though there is nobody at the window.

“Right,” she says. “Goodness.”

“I didn't know it had been sold.”

“Oh.”

“That's not your fault.”

I say it because her hand has gone to the gate as if she ought to do something with it. She takes the hand away. Leo stands beside me, close enough that our sleeves touch, not close enough that I can lean on him without making it deliberate.

“You've taken out the hedge,” I say.

“Yes. It was right across the path. We tried cutting it back, but it went very bare.”

“It always did.”

I can remember complaining about that hedge, about the leaves it dropped and the way it grew into the hinges of the gate. I want it back with a force which has nothing to do with whether it was a good hedge.

Hazel bends and lifts the edge of a bag of compost. It tears further down the side and a stream of dark crumbs runs onto the paving.

“Oh, for—”

She stops, glances at us and laughs without amusement.

“I've been putting off moving that because it would do exactly that.”

“You've got the empty pot,” I say.

“It's cracked.”

“A bucket?”

“Round the back.”

I open the gate. The loop of wire catches, so I have to stop and lift it off carefully. I am already inside when I realise I haven't been invited. I stay with one foot near the gate and point to the bag.

“I can hold that while you fetch it. If you want.”

She looks at the spilling compost. “That would help. Thank you.”

I crouch and lift the torn side, pinching the plastic together. It is damp underneath. Hazel goes along the side passage. Leo stays by the gate, then comes in far enough to close it behind him. He doesn't look towards the windows.

From here I can see the old mark on the stone step where we dropped a flowerpot. The shallow chip has filled with dirt. I used to scrub the step in spring, intending to keep it that colour all summer, and by June it would look the same as before. I want to put my palm against the stone, but I am holding the compost bag shut. The job saves me from choosing.

Hazel returns with two buckets. We scoop the spilled compost into one and lift the damaged bag over the other. Leo takes the bottom while I hold the tear closed. Together we lower it far enough that Hazel can tip the loose compost out without losing more across the path.

“That'll do,” she says. “I should have done it before I started.”

We set the bucket down. My fingers are black along the creases. Hazel offers me a rag, and I wipe the worst off them.

“We've put beds round the back,” she says. “Not much lawn left now.”

I look towards the side passage. She looks too, then back at me.

“You're welcome to see the garden. If you'd like. I can't offer tea; I've dismantled half the kitchen this morning.”

“The garden would be nice.”

Leo follows us along the passage. I used to keep a bag of road salt there and a broken watering can whose handle I intended to mend. Both are gone. The drain cover is the same. There is a new latch on the other gate, set higher than the old one, and Hazel reaches up to open it.

The back garden is full. Wooden beds take up most of the grass, with narrow paths between them. There are beans on a frame, small plants I cannot name, and a row of plastic labels rocking in the breeze. The washing line is in the same place but has been lowered at one end. A pair of work trousers hangs from it by the waistband.

I stand where the lawn used to begin. There was a path I wore into it going to the line. I can remember the feel of the grass through thin slippers, wet against my toes. Now there is a bed of broad leaves and a little board laid across the mud.

“Mind that,” Hazel says. “It tilts.”

I put my foot somewhere else.

She shows me the beans. Some have been eaten down to the stem. She points at one which has escaped and then at another which is beyond hope. Her annoyance is plain and particular. She has planted things, and something has come to eat them. I understand it without effort.

“I used to put the pots up on bricks,” I say. “Made no difference.”

“I tried that last year.”

“I thought the extra height might discourage them.”

She laughs. “They've got all night.”

For a moment I can laugh too. Then I look at the kitchen window. It is open a few inches. On the sill inside is a wooden object with holes in it, perhaps a knife block turned sideways. Nothing beyond it is clear. I move one step, and Hazel turns with me.

“I really have made a mess in there,” she says.

“I'm not coming in.”

“I didn't mean—”

“No. It's all right.”

I look back at the beans. It isn't all right in any useful sense, but I do not need her to make it so. Leo is standing by the washing line, trying not to brush the trousers with his shoulder. He once mowed this lawn while Jo and I sat on the step and gave him unnecessary instructions. His hair was dark then. He had a yellow shirt he wore until its collar frayed. I had forgotten the shirt entirely until this moment, and now I want to ask what happened to it.

Hazel follows my gaze to Leo.

“The apple tree's still here,” she says.

It is, against the far fence, crooked and bigger than I remember. The branches have been cut back, not brutally. Little green leaves shine at their ends. I go towards it, passing between the beds. A low branch is at the height of my face. I touch one leaf with the back of my finger, then take my hand away.

“It never gave us much,” I say.

“It gives us enough to argue with the birds.”

She goes to lift a watering can. I take it before she has to carry it around me, then give it to her on the other side of the path. Water shifts inside with a hollow sound. It is such an ordinary exchange that I feel, for a second, as if I have come to a neighbour's garden and will shortly go home to my own.

“Thank you for letting us look,” I say.

“Of course.”

We walk back down the passage. I don't turn towards the window again. At the front gate Hazel points to the wire loop.

“Could you put that over when you go? The latch doesn't always catch.”

I lift the wire over the post. It takes two attempts. Then I give the gate a little pull to check it. Hazel thanks me and goes back to her buckets.

In the car Leo waits before starting the engine.

“Are you all right?”

“No.”

He nods. I rub the remaining dirt from one finger onto another. I have brought out no leaf, no piece of wood, nothing I can pretend she offered me. There is a smear of compost on the side of my hand, and that will have to come off as well.

“I should have told you sooner,” he says.

“Yes.”

“I'm sorry.”

I look through the windscreen. At the next house a window is open, and someone inside pulls a curtain away from it. The fabric moves once in the air and settles.

“I'd like to wash my hands.”

“We can do that at mine.”

“Your house?”

“It's a cottage. Smaller.”

I turn towards him. I have not asked about it. The morning has held enough new buildings already, but he is waiting, and this one is where he lives.

“All right,” I say. “Show me.”
