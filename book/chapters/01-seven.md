# 1. Seven

The zip has caught a piece of the lining. I can reach the little metal tongue behind my waist, but whenever I pull, the whole dress pulls with it.

“Let me,” the attendant says.

She frees it with a fingernail. Her hands are warm. I turn to thank her and find myself holding one of them.

“Sorry.”

“That's all right.”

I let go. The dress is green, with yellow stitching around the pockets. I chose it because it didn't crease. Fifteen years ago I spent an afternoon deciding whether being dead would count as an occasion for the good dress, and chose this one instead.

The attendant puts my shoes on the floor. “Your twenty-four hours started at seven this morning. At seven tomorrow your return is over. There'll be a car wherever you are.”

“Even if we're out?”

“Wherever you are.”

“And I can't stay longer?”

“No. And there's no second return.”

We have both been through this before, though she was probably a child when I filled in the arrangements. I put my feet into the shoes. The left one makes a little farting noise against my heel. I lift it and try again, as if anyone here is likely to be offended.

Leo is waiting on a bench beyond the changing rooms. He has a blue shopping bag between his shoes. When he sees me he gets up, sits down, and gets up again. The bag falls over.

I had prepared for grey hair. There was grey in it already. I hadn't prepared for the loose skin below his chin, or for his eyebrows, which appear to have been left in charge of themselves. His mouth is pressed shut. I know that mouth. I go to it.

He holds me so carefully that I have to pull him closer. His coat smells of rain and something sharp, perhaps a cleaner he has started using. I push my face against his neck and look for the smell beneath it.

“You're here,” he says.

“Yes.”

He says it again. I kiss the part of his cheek I can reach. His glasses press into my forehead.

“Leo, your glasses.”

He takes them off without releasing me, which is difficult. We have a small struggle over whose arm goes where. Then he's laughing and wiping his face with the hand that holds the glasses, and I take them before he hurts himself.

On the bench I clean a lens with the hem of my dress. There is a smear on the other one that won't come off.

“I brought some things,” he says.

The bag contains trousers, a white shirt, socks still joined by a plastic tag, and a pair of soft shoes. He has folded the trousers around the shoes to keep them apart from the shirt.

“You didn't think I'd come dressed?”

“I thought you might want something else.”

I hold the shirt against me. It's a useful shirt. You could wear it to go somewhere you didn't especially want to go.

“It's your size,” he says.

“I haven't had much chance to change it.”

He looks at me. I put the shirt back.

“That was supposed to be funny.”

“I know.”

“You don't have to like it.”

“I know that too.”

He closes the bag, opens it to straighten a sleeve, closes it again. I touch the back of his hand. There are brown marks on it. One is the shape of a little boat. I want to examine every alteration, and I want him to stop watching me examine them.

“Breakfast,” I say.

“I've got things at home.”

“Rose's.”

“Flora.”

“Is it still there?”

“Yes.”

“Then Rose's.”

He takes a breath. I know the breath as well. An explanation is getting itself ready, with several good reasons arranged in order. I slide my fingers into his coat pocket. There's a tissue, a receipt, and his hand.

“A pot of tea,” I say. “Those enormous eggs.”

“Eggs are much the same size everywhere.”

“Not at Rose's. Everyone knows that.”

He moves his thumb across my fingers. I lean against him, lightly, so he can still decide to get up.

“You can have my tomato.”

“You always gave me your tomato.”

“A fine tradition. We shouldn't let it die out.”

This time he laughs. I keep still until he stands, taking me with him. He carries the bag in his other hand.

Outside, the air is colder than I expect. A delivery driver is trying to shut a van door on a box that's too long. I wait to see whether he solves it. Leo waits with me. The driver turns the box sideways and the door shuts. He doesn't see us applauding.

“We could take something home for Jo,” I say. “Does she still like those buns with the pink—”

“I don't know.”

“You don't know?”

He lets go of my hand to search for his keys.

“She doesn't eat breakfast with me very often.”

“No, of course. I meant for when she comes.”

He has found the keys but keeps them in his fist. Behind us the door opens. A woman comes out alone and stands looking at the road. I move aside for her, though she hasn't tried to pass.

“What time is she coming?” I ask.

“She said she'd try to come for half an hour.”

“Half an hour before what?”

“Just half an hour.”

I wait. He turns one key over with his thumb.

“It's her birthday,” I say, because perhaps one of us has forgotten how we arrived at this date.

“She's thirty.”

I know she's thirty. I chose thirty. At fifteen she was so certain that thirty meant finished that I thought it would be funny to arrive and catch her in the middle of being it.

“Have you got her number?”

“Yes.”

“I'll call from the café.”

He opens his mouth. I squeeze his arm before the explanation can come.

“After tea. I'm not going to ambush her before breakfast.”

I take the blue bag so he can find the car. It's heavier than it looks. While he leads me across the road, I rehearse how casually I can say hello.

* * *

Rose's has a step where I remember level ground. I put my foot down too soon and Leo catches my elbow. The bag hits the door with the sound of shoes being delivered.

Inside, there are small tables instead of the long counter. A woman in an apron moves a chair for the bag. I sit on the chair meant for it and have to get up again.

“Tea,” I say. “A pot.”

“For two?”

“Yes,” Leo says.

“I can still answer.”

“She was asking me.”

He takes his coat off. His jumper has a darn beneath one arm, very neat, in a blue that doesn't match.

We order eggs. They come in little iron pans, with spinach underneath. There is no tomato to give him.

I lift my knife. It feels heavier than a knife should, until I discover I am holding two stuck together. I separate them and give Leo the spare.

He has brought an envelope out of his coat. He lays it beside his plate, away from a drop of tea.

“What's that?”

“Six photographs.”

“Only six?”

“I had to stop somewhere.”

The top one shows Leo in a small boat. He wears an orange life jacket and holds a wooden object above his head.

“Did you catch it?”

“It's a paddle.”

“I can see that.”

“You looked doubtful.”

I turn the photograph over. September, last year. His handwriting has become larger.

“I made the boat,” he says.

“You made a boat?”

“Mostly. There are kits.”

He takes the picture to show me a joint. His nail rests on a little dark line in the wood.

“It leaked there the first time. I thought I'd done the outside badly, but it was coming through from—”

“Could I have the phone before we get engrossed?”

His nail stays on the photograph.

“You said after tea.”

“I've had tea.”

He looks at my cup. I take a swallow. It scalds the roof of my mouth and I have to keep my lips closed while I breathe through my nose.

Leo puts the photographs back in the envelope. He brings up Jo's number, then lays the phone in my palm. It is warm and much too thin. I have the unpleasant feeling that I could bend it by wanting something too hard.

It rings four times.

“Dad? Is that the measurement?”

“It's me.”

Something scrapes at her end.

“Jo?”

“I know.”

I look across at Leo. He has put on his glasses to read the small print on the jar of marmalade.

“Happy birthday,” I say.

“Thank you.”

Her voice is lower. I recognise the way she breathes before speaking, but the voice arriving after it keeps surprising me.

“Are you having a nice morning?”

“I'm under a table.”

“Oh.”

“I haven't fallen. I'm looking for woodworm.”

The sound I make is too loud. The woman at the next table looks up.

“Have you found any?”

“There's a hole. It might just be a hole.”

“Most of them are.”

I hear her laugh once, very close to the phone. Then she moves it and says something to somebody else.

“I told Dad about it,” she says. “A round table. For the flat. Mine folds down if you lean on the wrong bit.”

“On your birthday?”

“The man selling it doesn't keep a calendar of my birthdays.”

“No. I suppose he doesn't.”

“He'll deliver, but I need to decide. I'm having people over tonight and I would like us all to put our elbows down.”

“How many people?”

“Six. Seven if Nina brings Patrick. She keeps saying he probably won't, which usually means he will.”

I look at the envelope beside Leo's plate. Six photographs. Seven people I don't know.

“Your dad said you might come.”

“I said I'd try.”

“For half an hour.”

“Yes.”

I press the phone harder against my ear, as if she is too far away for a different reason.

“I don't know what I'm supposed to do with half an hour, Jo.”

“Neither do I.”

For a moment there is only café noise. Leo cuts a piece of toast into two narrow strips.

“Where are you?” she asks.

“Rose's.”

“I could come there at about eleven. If this gets sorted.”

“Or I could come to the table.”

“You want to come here?”

“Well, you seem to be staying.”

“I have to pay before he'll put it in the van. But I don't want you to come all this way and spend it discussing furniture.”

“Is it a very long way?”

“Harper Street. The warehouse behind the tyre place.”

I put my hand over the phone and ask Leo. He nods, then holds his hand out. I hesitate before giving it to him.

“The doorway's seventy-four,” he says. “But the hall turns. Did you ask about taking the legs off?”

He listens.

“No, don't let him tell you it'll fit because it's round. Get him to turn it over.”

He passes the phone back.

“I'll come,” I say.

“If you want.”

“I do.”

“I've got to go with the delivery afterwards.”

“Yes.”

“And I haven't got any cake.”

“I haven't asked you for cake.”

“I know. I'm telling you.”

We say goodbye. I look at the lit glass until Leo reaches over and ends the call.

My eggs have a skin on them. I push my chair back.

“Shall we?”

“I'd like to eat this.”

“Of course.”

I sit. He picks up one of the strips of toast, then puts it down.

“And I'd like to finish telling you about the boat.”

“I am listening.”

“You were. Until you weren't.”

The woman brings more hot water. We both thank her with such care that she checks whether we need anything else.

“I've got until tomorrow morning,” I say when she has gone.

“I know how long you've got.”

He takes the envelope off the table. I catch the flap before he can put it away. For a second we are each holding an end.

“Bring them.”

“I did.”

“To Jo's table. If it fits.”

His thumb presses a dent in the paper. Then he lets go.

I eat the spinach, which is cold enough to handle but still unpleasant to swallow. Leo tells me that the paddle in the picture belongs to a woman called Bess. His own is on the bottom of the river. He wasn't celebrating in the photograph. He was asking for another one.

I laugh with egg in my mouth and he has to wait for me to finish.

Outside, I put the envelope into the pocket with the yellow stitching. The bag of clothes is between my feet while Leo unlocks the car. I want Jo to be pleased when I arrive. I also want that table to have woodworm, just enough to keep her there until we reach her.

I pick up the bag before I can make a joke of it.
