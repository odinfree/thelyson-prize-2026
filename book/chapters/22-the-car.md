# 22. The car

At twenty to seven Leo gets up to lock the shed. He asks whether we have left anything inside. Jo says no. I think of the needle in its cork, the cloths laid out to dry, the crooked stitch in the leather. None of those things needs fetching.

We wait while he checks the door. He gives it two pulls, one with each hand. Jo watches him and says nothing.

The place is beginning to belong to the day. A car passes on the road. Somewhere behind the sheds a radio starts, then stops, then starts more quietly. I can smell coffee from Jo's open flask and a faint oily smell from the little engine of a boat farther along the bank.

“We should go up to the gate,” Leo says.

“They said wherever I am.”

“I know.”

The gate is easier for a car than the bench by the water. That is all he means. I stand and rub the cold place on the back of my trousers. Jo picks up the flask and the paper from the cake. She folds the paper several times, making it much smaller than it needs to be.

By Leo's car I take off the canvas jacket. The air reaches my arms through the shirt at once.

“Keep it on,” he says.

“It's yours.”

“I know whose jacket it is.”

I put it on again. We have not gained much by the discussion except another small familiar annoyance, and I am pleased enough with that to smile.

He opens the back door and lifts out the blue bag. I take it from him. My dress is on top, a piece of yellow stitching visible where the fabric has shifted. I smooth it down and check that the shoes are not pressing against the photographs.

“You keep this,” I say.

“I thought—”

“I'll stay in these.”

I touch the shirt, the trousers. The sensible shoes are wet at one edge from the landing. They feel entirely mine now, though he chose and bought them. The green dress can go back to his cottage or somewhere else. I don't tell him what to do with it.

“The pictures are in the little pocket.”

“Yes.”

I unzip it and take them out, not because he doubts me but because I want to see that the corners are straight. They are. Six photographs, still together. I put them back and close the zip to the end.

“Don't lose the tomato,” I say.

“There's a copy.”

“Of the tomato?”

“The photograph.”

“Good. A reserve crop.”

He takes the bag. I have nothing to carry. It is a peculiar sensation after a day of bags, plates, a table leg, the oar handles. I put my hands into the jacket pockets. There is a small round stone in one, smooth on one side and rough on the other. I bring it out.

“Yours?”

Leo looks at it. “Apparently.”

“You don't know?”

“I pick things up.”

I put it back. I cannot spend the last part of the morning solving every object.

Jo is looking down the lane. I hear the car before I see it, tyres passing slowly over the rough place near the entrance. It is a grey estate car, cleaner than Leo's, with a small white card in the windscreen. The driver stops short of the gate, turns the engine off and gets out.

She is not the attendant from yesterday. She has short dark hair and a coat buttoned wrongly at the bottom. She notices the button while coming towards us, stops to correct it and then says my name.

“Yes.”

“Good morning. I'm Ruth.”

“Good morning.”

She looks at the time. “There's no hurry yet. I'll be just here.”

She indicates the car. I nod as if this were an arrangement about taking me to a station. Perhaps it is easier for her if I nod. Perhaps she takes no special notice of what I do.

She goes back and opens the rear door. Then she stands beside the front wing, looking at the river rather than at us.

Jo takes my hand out of the jacket pocket. The stone stays behind.

“I thought it would be the same woman,” I say.

Jo looks towards Ruth but does not answer.

There is no reason it should have been. People have shifts, homes to go to, things they do when they aren't helping other people leave. I am irritated by the reasonableness of this and say nothing about it.

Leo has put the blue bag back into his car. He shuts the door without quite enough force. It stays on the first catch. He opens it and shuts it again.

“Are you cold?” he asks.

“A little.”

He looks at the jacket I am already wearing. There is nothing further to put around me. He comes close instead and rests his hand on the back of my neck, above the collar. His thumb moves once behind my ear.

I am frightened. I have told them this and it is still true. I want to get into Leo's car, with the bag on my lap and his unreliable heater beginning to work. I want to hear him decide whether to turn left at the lights. The wanting is so precise that I can feel the weight of the bag against my knees.

Jo squeezes my hand.

“I'm glad I came,” she says.

I look at her face. She is not offering to make the evening different. She is here, tired, with a small spot beside her eye and her hair flattened at the back.

“So am I.”

“I mean now. This morning.”

“I know.”

I put my other arm around her and she comes against me. There is no bag between us this time. I can hold her without moving anything aside. Her face is against my shoulder; I feel the heat of her breath through the shirt and jacket. I put my mouth to her hair and leave it there.

When she moves, I let her. She keeps hold of my hand for another moment, then gives it to Leo as if it were something he had asked her to pass.

He doesn't take it. He puts both arms around me instead.

I can feel how tired he is. Not in a grand way. His shoulders are stiff, and he has not slept enough, and one foot moves to take pressure off the knee. I move with him until we are comfortable. He presses his cheek against mine.

“Thank you for the boat,” I say.

“You did most of the work.”

“Badly.”

“Some of it.”

I laugh once, against his neck. He holds me tighter, then less tightly. Neither is right for long.

Across his shoulder I can see Ruth checking the car door. There is a yellow leaf caught in the rubber at the bottom. She picks it out and drops it on the ground. The gesture is so quick that I nearly miss it.

“It's time to get in,” she says.

Leo lets his arms open. I step back before I can ask him to hold me again. Jo comes beside me, then stops when I stop. We have not rehearsed who walks how far.

“Here,” I say.

She nods, though I am not sure whether I am telling her where to wait or telling myself where I am. I touch her cheek with the backs of my fingers. Then I turn towards the car.

The rear seat is lower than I expect. I sit too soon and have to bring one foot in after the rest of me. The jacket catches beneath my leg. I pull it free, impatient with a difficulty I ought to know how to solve.

“All right?” Ruth asks.

“One moment.”

She waits. I find the belt and bring it across. The metal tongue misses the catch. I try again. This time it clicks.

“There.”

Ruth closes the door gently. I see Leo's hand move towards it and stop. Jo is beside him. She has tucked the empty cake paper into her coat pocket; one white corner sticks out. Ruth goes round to the front, gets in and fastens her belt. The engine starts with a small vibration beneath my feet.

Inside the car there is the clean smell of upholstery and somebody's mint. A small bottle of water sits in the pocket of the door. I move it because it is rattling against something hard. Then I put my hand flat against the window.

Jo raises hers. She doesn't put it on the glass. She holds it a little way away, opposite mine. Her fingers are spread, and there is the dark line beneath one nail that I saw on the bench.

The dashboard clock changes to seven. I am looking at that finger, at the little crescent she has missed.
