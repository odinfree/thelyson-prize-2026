# 18. A message

I wait for sleep as if it has an appointment with me. Leo has gone without waiting. His hand is between us, palm up, fingers bent. I put one finger into the hollow and he closes his hand around it without waking.

The room is quiet enough for me to hear a motor starting somewhere downstairs. A fridge. It stops, and the quiet after it seems louder than the motor did.

I should sleep. I have been awake since before seven, or at least I have been awake for everything I remember since then. My legs are tired. There is a sore place beside one thumbnail. If I close my eyes, the afternoon's bright wood appears behind them: the table on its trestles, the boat on its pads, the bit of chipped edge I caused. None of it keeps still long enough to be a dream.

I withdraw my finger. Leo's hand closes and stays closed.

The white shirt is on the chair. I put it on, buttoning only enough of it to keep the front together, and find the trousers by feeling along the floor with my foot. The new socks are inside out. I leave them that way when I pull them on.

On the landing I stop, waiting to remember why I have got up. Water. A message. Some small preparation that might make the morning less sudden.

The stair nearest the bottom creaks. I knew it did this afternoon, when I came up to change, but I have forgotten which one it is. I find it with all my weight.

In the kitchen the little clock above the cooker says half past twelve. I get a glass of water and drink it standing at the sink. Outside, a security light comes on in somebody else's garden. The fence appears, a watering can, three dark pots. Then they go away.

There is a pad on the counter, beside a pencil with its rubber worn down to the metal. The top page is a list: washing liquid, bird food, screws. Under screws Leo has written a size, crossed it out and written another. I turn to a clean page.

Jo,

I look at the name. I have written it often enough: on bags for school, on the inside of a shoe, on a card she took no notice of until she had examined the present. My hand can still make it in one quick movement.

Underneath I write, I am so glad.

I stop. Glad about what? That she has reached thirty? That she has a good table? That she can ask an accompanist to begin again when he gets it wrong? I can feel a whole page arranging itself around the version of her I would like to leave behind, and I do not trust it.

I put a line through the words. It cuts the tail off the g in glad. I turn the page, then turn it back because hiding the first attempt seems wasteful.

The pencil makes a little grey mark on the side of my hand. I rub it with my thumb. Upstairs something moves. A board, then a cough.

Leo appears in the doorway wearing his dressing gown. It is a darker blue than the bag and missing its belt.

“What are you doing?”

“Making a mess of your list.”

He looks at the pad. “That's what lists are for.”

“I was writing to Jo.”

He goes to the sink, fills the glass I have just used and drinks from it. “Is she still up?”

“I don't know.”

“Probably.”

I look at the clock. “Would it be awful to call?”

“You could ask.”

“That's rather the point of calling.”

He smiles sleepily. “Message first. I'll get the phone.”

He goes back upstairs and I tear the page off the pad. The sound is louder than I intend. When he returns, I have folded it into a small square. He doesn't ask to see it.

“Here. I've opened Jo.”

Her name is at the top of the screen. Below it are messages about ordinary things, the last one giving the time he would meet me. I make myself look only at the blank place where I am supposed to put new words.

“It's Flora,” I say as I type. “Are you still awake?”

“She'll know it's you.”

“It says Dad.”

“Fair point.”

I send it. He shows me where to put the phone down so it won't balance on the pencil, then pulls out the other chair and sits. He is pale with sleep, his hair sticking up where I have had my hand in it.

“Go back,” I say. “I'll bring it.”

“I'm all right.”

The screen lights. Yes. Everyone's gone. You okay?

The question loosens something in my chest. I type, Can I call? and almost immediately the phone begins to ring in my hand.

Leo points to the part I should touch. I do. Jo says my name, though not the one I have typed.

“Mum?”

“Hello.”

“What is it?”

“Nothing terrible.”

There is a silence in which I hear how a call after midnight must arrive. “I'm sorry. I should have put that in the message.”

“No. It's fine. I was just—”

A scraping noise.

“What are you doing?”

“Opening the window. It's still hot in here.”

I can picture the handle. I watched her use it earlier. At last she is in a room I know as she knows it, without having to supply an old room for her voice.

“Did they stay long?”

“Until about twelve. Arun came back for the dish he'd left. Twice, actually. First he came back for the wrong dish.”

“He had a busy day.”

“He did.”

Leo gets up. He touches my shoulder as he passes, then goes back upstairs. I turn the phone slightly so Jo won't hear the creaking step and wonder if I am trying to conceal him leaving a room in his own house.

“Are you in bed?” I ask.

“No. I'm putting things away. I don't know why. They'd still be here in the morning.”

“I liked Mara.”

“She liked you.”

“I may have made her uncomfortable in the kitchen.”

“She recovered. She had more cake.”

I look at the folded piece of paper beneath my glass. I have put it there without noticing, to keep water off the table. A round damp patch is beginning to pass through it.

“I was trying to write you a message.”

“You did.”

“On paper. Something for afterwards.”

She doesn't speak for a moment. Then a cupboard shuts.

“You can, if you want.”

“I couldn't think what to say.”

“You don't have to do it now.”

We both hear the mistake. She begins to correct it and I say, “I know what you mean.”

Outside, a car comes along the lane slowly. I watch its lights pass across the kitchen window, showing each smear I have left on the glass when I stood there earlier. I can feel Jo waiting. She has always been able to wait when she doesn't want to help me past something.

“I'm frightened,” I say.

The words leave me without any improvement. I had hoped that saying them might at least make them more exact.

“About the morning?”

“Yes.”

“About what happens?”

“About having to stop doing this.”

She moves the phone. Her breathing is closer when she speaks again. “I don't know what to say.”

“You needn't say anything.”

“That's not much use either.”

“It's all right.”

“No, it isn't.”

I take the paper from under the glass. The water has softened the crease. It begins to tear when I unfold it, so I leave it folded.

“No,” I say. “It isn't.”

There is a long pause. I hear a little clink at her end and ask what she has put down.

“A spoon. I keep carrying it round. Every time I think I've finished, I'm still holding it.”

I begin to laugh, quietly, and she does too. The spoon is no answer to anything. I am grateful to it anyway.

We talk about the morning. I tell her the landing, below the shed, and six o'clock. Leo thinks we can have a short row before then if the water is suitable. She knows the path from the road, the wide gate and the loose stone near the bottom.

“You went with him,” I say.

“Last summer. Twice.”

“He told me.”

“I wasn't keeping it from you.”

“I know.”

And I do. I have been absent from too many things to make each one a secret.

“I may come,” she says. “I need to sleep.”

“Yes.”

“Set your alarm before you forget.”

“Your dad has.”

“Of course he has.”

There is affection in the way she says it, and something worn beside it. I almost ask. She speaks first.

“He sent me another flat.”

“What?”

“A link. Somewhere to rent. Ground floor, easier to heat. He does it when I mention the boiler, or the stairs, or anything wrong with this place.”

“Was it nicer?”

“That's not the point.”

I lean my forehead on my hand. Even now I can walk directly into an answer she has given somebody else a hundred times.

“No,” I say. “It isn't.”

“I like my flat. I can complain about a tap without wanting another kitchen.”

“Have you said?”

“Yes.”

I leave it there. I do not undertake to talk to him. She has not asked me to, and he is upstairs with the alarm set, asleep or trying to be.

We say goodbye three times, each finding a small thing after the previous one. The last is mine: “The pan.”

“What about it?”

“Did it come clean?”

“No. I've left it.”

“Good.”

“Go to bed, Mum.”

“You too.”

I end the call myself. The screen goes back to her name. I put the phone on the counter and rinse my glass, then dry it because I don't know whether Leo prefers it in the cupboard or the drainer. I put it in the drainer anyway.

The paper has dried at the edges. I open it carefully. Jo. I am so glad, crossed out. I cannot leave her those words and their failure. I tear the page into small pieces and put them in the bin beneath the sink. Then I put the pencil back beside washing liquid and screws.

Upstairs I return the phone to Leo's table. The alarm display is already there. I do not touch it. He shifts when I get in and reaches towards me, his hand landing first on the cover and then on my arm.

“All right?”

“She's tired.”

“So are you.”

I lie down facing him. The room is darker than it was, or my eyes are slower. I can make out his nose against the pillow. I listen until his breathing lengthens again, then stop trying to hear each separate breath.
