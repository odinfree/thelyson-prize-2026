# 12. Eight

Nina arrives carrying two chairs and arguing with a man I take to be Patrick before she introduces him. He has a bag in each hand. She tells him he could have put one down and taken a chair, and he says he offered outside. They stop when they see me, leaving the end of the argument in the doorway.

“Flora,” Jo says. “Nina. And Patrick.”

Nina turns sideways to get the chairs through. “I would shake your hand, but I appear to be moving in.”

I take one. It is heavier than it looks, cold where the metal has been resting against her coat. Patrick puts the bags down and reaches for the other, too late to be useful to her. She thanks him anyway. It sounds like an arrangement they have made over many years.

“You came,” Jo says to him.

“Yes. Is that all right?”

“Entirely unexpected. I've only counted you four times.”

“I told Nina I was coming.”

Nina looks at the ceiling. He kisses Jo's cheek and takes off his jacket. There is a pale patch of plaster dust on one cuff.

“I've eaten,” he says.

“Good,” Jo says. “You can watch.”

He looks briefly concerned. She pats his arm and sends him into the sitting room with the bags.

We unfold the chairs beside the tables. One opens at once; the other stops halfway. Nina and I each try the same thing, then turn it over. A little hook has been bent against the frame. She holds it steady while I lift the hook with the blunt end of a key Jo gives us. It releases with a bang which makes Ellen call from the kitchen.

“Nobody,” Jo calls back, before her aunt can ask.

We set the chair down and Nina sits on it experimentally. She bounces once, very slightly, then stands and puts it at the folding table.

“Patrick's,” she says.

“That seems fair.”

She grins at me. Her fingernails are painted green except for one, which is purple. She sees me looking and holds up the hand.

“Couldn't decide.”

“Still deciding?”

“It's been a week. I think this may be the decision.”

The doorbell rings again. Jo goes downstairs, and Nina watches her leave with a familiarity I have not yet earned. Then she turns back to the table, runs a hand over its surface and bends to look beneath it.

“Good purchase.”

“She did a lot of looking,” I say.

“She usually does.”

“I helped carry it.”

“I heard.”

Her hand stops near the new chip. I cannot tell whether that is what she heard about. I consider telling her before Jo does, getting the laugh in first. Instead I put the key beside the bowl with the pencils.

Mara comes in with a soft parcel tucked beneath one arm. She is wearing a yellow coat open over a black top, and her hair has a dent from the clip she has just taken out. She kisses Jo, says happy birthday twice because they speak over one another, then sees Ellen and raises a hand.

“You made it,” Ellen says.

“Eventually.”

The parcel is a scarf, folded round a book to give it enough shape to wrap. Mara explains this while Jo takes off the paper. The book is not new; a ticket falls out of it and Mara retrieves it before anyone can read it.

“That part wasn't included.”

Jo opens the book in the middle and begins to laugh. Mara bends beside her, both their heads nearly touching. I can see a drawing, something with wheels, but not enough of the page to understand. Their laughter does not need me. I wait, feeling foolish for waiting, until Jo closes the book and puts it on the sideboard.

“Mara, this is Mum.”

Mara's hand is warm from holding the parcel. She looks at me directly, then smiles in a way which seems to decide something for herself.

“Hello, Flora. Jo says you've had a very furniture-heavy day.”

“So far.”

“I was offered the lifting. I was conveniently at work.”

“What do you do?”

“Admin. With a great deal of walking between people who also do admin.”

I laugh. She takes off the yellow coat and hands it to Jo, who hangs it without asking where Mara wants it. That small confidence pleases me before I understand why. There are people here whose coats my daughter knows what to do with.

Phil rings from the street because the bell seems not to have worked. We hear Jo's phone, then him saying he is already outside, though she can see that. Patrick offers to go down. He comes back carrying a loaf wrapped in a tea towel, while Phil follows with his own coat over one arm.

“I'd have let him keep it,” Phil says, “but I wanted to see whether anyone could cut it.”

The loaf has split along the top, opening into a dark ridge. He baked it himself and has brought it without deciding whether it is a gift or an apology. Arun's borrowed bread is already on the counter. Jo says two sorts will be useful. Phil looks grateful enough that I like her for saying it.

He has a broad face and a small careful voice. When Jo introduces us he holds out a hand, changes his mind and leans forward, then stops again. I shake the hand while it is available. We both seem relieved to have completed something.

“Thank you for coming,” I say.

He looks at me, then at Jo, and I realise I have made myself the hostess.

“For her,” I add, which makes it worse.

“For the bread,” Jo says. “He's come to supervise it.”

Phil lifts the loaf. “It needs support.”

We let him take it into the kitchen. Jo touches the back of my wrist as she passes, a brief contact she need not have made. I let it mean something small.

There is a period in which everybody stands in the wrong place with something in a hand. Patrick opens a bottle and cannot find where to put the cap. Nina tries to give Jo a plant wrapped in paper while Jo is holding two glasses. Ellen has found napkins in a drawer and wants to know whether all of them can be used. Mara takes the glasses, I take the plant, Patrick puts the cap in his pocket. The room begins to work.

The plant has thick leaves and one awkward pink flower. I hold it level while Nina pulls away the paper. A little soil falls onto the table. She wipes it with her sleeve, and Jo catches her hand.

“Cloth.”

“It's only soil.”

“Then it will come off with a cloth.”

Nina accepts the cloth. I look at the pale chip on the opposite edge and feel a sudden, unreasonable fellowship with her.

Arun arrives last, carrying wine and a round plastic tub. He has changed his shirt but the crease from his apron remains across his trousers. He apologises to Jo for being late, to Nina for not answering a message, and to Ellen for putting the tub down where she has just wiped.

“Sit for a minute,” Jo says.

“I will.”

Instead he goes straight into the kitchen and opens the tub. Lentils, still faintly warm, with chopped onion and something green. He had meant to make a small amount and made the amount his hands expected at work. Tessa has kept the rest.

“The tub needs to go back,” he says.

“So does everything,” I tell him.

He laughs, looking at me for the first time. “How was the afternoon?”

“I've learned to row on dry land.”

“That sounds forgiving.”

“It stayed very still.”

His laugh brings Nina into the doorway. She asks where we went, and I tell her about the shed and the needle and how Leo's boat looks longer when you have to imagine moving it. I do not mention Catherine. I am pleased to have something new of my own to tell.

Jo comes to check the oven and finds the three of us blocking it. We move in separate directions. She laughs at the choreography and takes out the dish, then puts it back because it has not yet warmed through. I want to ask whether I should have turned it up earlier. She is already checking the rice.

“Ten minutes,” she says. “Have a drink.”

“Is there anything—”

“Have a drink, Mum.”

I take the glass Arun offers me. Wine, pale and cold. I taste it too quickly and it stings the place I burnt at breakfast. My body has kept that small account all day without needing to be reminded.

In the other room Ellen is telling Phil how she knows the street he lives on. She names a shop that is no longer there. He names the one which replaced it, and they find common ground in disliking the changes to the crossing. I recognise this version of my sister: elbows drawn in, voice a little higher, making certain everybody has heard the beginning of the story before she arrives at its end.

Nina asks me where I would like to sit. I say anywhere, then immediately look for the place beside Jo. Nina follows my look, moves a glass and makes room. She does it without comment. I am grateful and slightly annoyed to have been easy to read.

We have six places round the new table and two at the old folding one, drawn close enough for conversation but not pretending to be the same piece of furniture. Arun takes one of the extra places. Patrick claims the other, lowering himself carefully onto Nina's repaired chair.

“Tested,” she tells him.

“By whom?”

“Someone lighter.”

He checks the hook, finds it secure and sits back. His knees fit beneath the table if he angles one leg out. Arun moves his chair a little so they will not collide. A plate placed at the near corner can be passed without anybody reaching across the gap.

Jo sits beside me to see whether we will all have room, then gets up again at once. Her apron catches the chair. I release the tie while she holds still, and for a moment I am close enough to see the little hairs at the back of her neck.

“There,” I say.

“Thanks.”

She goes back to the kitchen. I keep the apron tie between two fingers until it slides free.

Mara takes the seat across from me. She asks whether I want the window closed. It is open only a little, but a cool line of air reaches the backs of my hands. I say I would like it left. She nods and moves the plant away from the edge instead.

Nobody has yet asked me what it is like to be dead. I realise I have been waiting for this with a readiness almost indistinguishable from wanting it. These people have made several decisions before coming upstairs. I do not know which were Jo's instructions and which their own good sense.

“Do you mind people asking?” Phil says.

Ellen stops in the middle of reaching for her glass.

“Asking what?”

He looks at my face, then towards the kitchen. “About today.”

“I don't mind that.”

“What did you want to do first?”

I could say hold my husband, see my daughter, breathe the air. All of those would be true if I arranged them correctly.

“Have breakfast,” I say. “I was quite forceful about it.”

He smiles. “Good breakfast?”

“The eggs had changed.”

“How much can an egg change?” Patrick asks.

I look at him. “That's what Leo said.”

They laugh, and the sound moves round the two tables without anybody having to translate it. I tell them about the missing tomato and the two knives stuck together. Phil adds an account of a hotel which served him a poached egg in a cup with no spoon. Nina insists this was deliberate. Patrick asks whether he drank it. I am still laughing when Jo comes in carrying the dressing.

She puts the little bowl of the thicker version beside my place. No announcement. She merely places it where I can reach it and goes back for the dish.

I look at the bowl. Mara is asking Phil what happened to the egg, and Ellen is reaching over to move a glass out of the way. The room is warm now, crowded and not quite level. I put my hand flat on the table beside the small pale break in its edge and wait for Jo to bring the food.
