# 17. The photographs

He kisses me before either of us has shut the door. Cold air presses against my back. I reach behind me, miss the handle and have to stop kissing him to find it.

“Something practical first,” he says.

“The whole day has been practical.”

“Take your shoes off, then.”

I lean against the wall and pull at a heel. The shoe resists. I have spent the evening admiring it and now it won't leave me alone. Leo gets down on one knee, makes an unhappy noise about the knee and takes both shoes off in quick succession.

“You should have asked,” I say.

“For permission?”

“For a chair.”

He laughs against my leg. I put my hand in his hair. It is thicker at the back than on top, warm where the collar has kept it. When he stands, I leave my hand at his neck.

In the kitchen his supper pan is beside the sink. He has put water in it. A single plate stands in the drainer and a fork lies across it. The arrangement gives me an unreasonable pleasure. While I was with Jo, he was here eating what he wanted, and I can still come into the kitchen and see the result.

“Drink?” he asks.

“Water.”

He takes two glasses from the cupboard. I sit and find the canvas jacket too bulky behind my back. I take it off and fold it over the next chair, then put the blue bag on top of it. My shirt is creased across the waist. One cuff has a little red mark, probably tomato. I rub it with a damp finger and spread it.

“Leave it,” he says. “I'll wash it.”

“You won't need to.”

He puts the glass down too sharply. Water goes onto the table.

I reach for the cloth beside the sink. “Sorry.”

“You can leave a shirt.”

“Yes.”

“That's all I meant.”

I wipe the spill. The cloth is soft from use, with a hole near one edge. I want to talk about the cloth. I want to ask him whether he has more of them and whether he cuts up old towels. Anything with an answer small enough to be completed.

Instead I unzip the pocket inside the bag and bring out the envelope.

“We haven't done these properly.”

“We don't have to do them properly.”

“No. But I'd like to look.”

I put the six photographs on the dry part of the table. The top one is the boat again. Leo with his mouth open, the borrowed paddle above his head. I can see the joke in it now without his explanation. I recognise the boat's blue side, and beyond it the low bank where we have arranged to meet Jo.

“Who took it?”

“Bess. Standing on the landing.”

“While you were asking for help.”

“She thought I'd be pleased to have a record.”

I lay it beside the envelope. His pleasure in choosing it has become clearer to me than the moment it records. He wanted to show me the boat and also this version of himself, ridiculous inside something he had managed to make.

Under it is Jo in a hard hat, holding what looks like a broken piece of ceiling. There are pale curls on it, interrupted where it has snapped. Her hands wear blue gloves. She is looking away from the person taking the photograph.

“Plaster,” he says.

“I can see.”

“You were turning it upside down.”

I turn it back. “You can look at plaster upside down.”

He moves his chair beside mine. “She sent me that. Something they found behind a later ceiling.”

“Where?”

He names a place I have never heard of, then admits he may have the wrong job. On the back he has written only Jo at work. I trace the capital J without touching it.

“You should write the place down.”

“I will when she tells me again.”

“She'll say she's told you.”

“She will.”

We are pleased by the same small certainty. I look at the hands holding the plaster. There is room in them for it. I have been watching Jo's face all day, trying to compare it with an old one, and now I find myself interested in how she supports this awkward thing without covering the part she wants somebody to see.

I put that photograph on the other side of the boat.

Catherine comes next. I have looked at her already and still prepare myself before turning the picture fully towards me. Her hood has blown back. Rain shines on Leo's glasses. Behind them a grey sea rises to a horizon that isn't level because whoever took the photograph held the camera badly.

“Did she like the boat?”

“Didn't much like being cold.”

“Sensible woman.”

He smiles a little. “She liked lots of things about it. The making. Buying the bits. Taking something back because I'd bought the wrong bit.”

“Did you make her take things back?”

“She preferred to. She was better at it.”

The answer is so ordinary that I find it easier to bear than a careful account of happiness. This was a woman who could stand in a shop with the wrong fitting and get the right one. She had a voice for that. I look at the wind lifting a strand of hair from her forehead.

“Have you told her about today?”

“Yes.”

“What did she say?”

He considers before answering. “She hoped it went well.”

I cannot tell whether those were all the words. I could ask. I don't. I put her beside Jo, and his hand, resting on the table, opens a little.

The next picture gives me Jo's flat before it belonged so thoroughly to her. Boxes stand behind the folding table. There is a packet of bread on it, two bowls, a lamp without a shade. Jo sits with one foot tucked under her. Her hair is longer than it is now and falling out of whatever she has done to hold it up.

“First night?”

“First supper there. Not the first night. She wanted the cooker working before she let anyone come.”

“That sounds reasonable.”

“We could have brought something.”

“It was her cooker.”

He nods, though I am not sure he agrees. At the corner of the photograph another person's hand reaches for the bread. I ask whose and he says he can't remember. Not every person cropped out of a photograph has vanished from his life; he simply cannot remember whose hand that is. I like the picture more for it.

The fifth shows muddy water outside a wide door. Bess stands on a dry strip of concrete holding one end of a chair, while Leo holds the other. There are more chairs behind them, upended, and somebody in yellow waterproof trousers is laughing with their head bent down.

“The club flooded.”

“That wasn't the bad one.”

“You have categories?”

“We've had practice.”

He points to a pale line on the wall. “We got most things above it. The water came up another few inches.”

I imagine their morning's work undone by those inches. I can see why he has kept the photograph: the chair is halfway between them, and neither can put it down without the other. But perhaps he has kept it because Bess is making a face, or because he likes those yellow trousers. I ask him.

“It was a good day,” he says.

“In a flood?”

“After. That was the cleaning up. The water had gone down.”

I look again. He is right. I have made a crisis out of the puddle beside them. The trousers are wet nearly to the waist, but the person in them is laughing.

The last photograph is of his tomatoes.

At first I fail to understand what he wants me to see. Three narrow plants lean against sticks in pots. Their leaves are sparse and yellow at the edges. On one stem hangs a single small green tomato. A card tied to the stick says ABUNDANCE in letters I recognise from Jo's receipt notes and the back of her hand.

I begin to laugh before I can stop myself. Leo takes the picture and examines it with the air of a man studying somebody else's crime.

“It ripened.”

“All by itself?”

“It was a reasonable size.”

“Did you share it?”

“I ate it standing there. I wasn't risking another disappointment.”

He gives me the photograph. I am still laughing when I put it down, and the laughter has nowhere graceful to end. It catches. My nose fills. I press the heel of my hand against it and Leo reaches across for a tissue from the box on the windowsill.

“I wanted to see them,” I say.

“The tomatoes?”

“Yes. The tomatoes.”

He gives me another tissue. I use it and put both in my lap. I can see the ruined plants in their small pots, making no effort at all to deserve this response.

“I'll grow some this year,” he says.

“You don't have to promise me vegetables.”

“I was going to anyway.”

“Good.”

He begins to collect the photographs. I stop him, touching his wrist, and keep the tomatoes in front of me while he puts the other five away. Then I hand it over too. I watch him slide it into the envelope, turn down the flap and give it to me.

“You keep them,” I say.

“They're mine.”

“I know. I mean—”

“I know what you mean.”

I put the envelope in the bag's inner pocket again. The zip catches a corner. I back it off and tuck the envelope farther in before closing it. Leo drinks the rest of his water.

Upstairs, the bedroom has one bedside lamp on. He must have put it on before he came to meet me. There is a book lying face down on the far pillow. I pick it up and look for a scrap of paper to mark his place.

“Just turn it over,” he says.

“You'll break the spine.”

“I've been breaking it for weeks.”

I use the clean tissue from my pocket and put the book on the little table. The bed is narrower than ours used to be. I sit on the edge and the mattress gives beneath me, making a small slope towards him when he sits too.

He touches the red mark on my cuff.

“Did you cut yourself?”

“Tomato.”

“Busy evening.”

I turn my wrist so his fingers go into my palm. We kiss without the door to attend to. His lips are dry. I touch the cut on his chin and he tells me not to pick it, which makes me kiss it instead.

We are awkward at undressing. My cuffs are still buttoned when I try to pull the shirt off, and his jumper catches his glasses. He puts them safely on the table before we do anything else. I want to laugh and not interrupt, and finally laugh because there is no useful way of keeping it in.

“You can look,” he says when I go quiet.

I am looking. His chest has changed, and the small hollow beneath his ribs. There is more grey hair than I expect and less of it in some places. I lay my hand against him. He is warm under the ordinary skin, and when I move my palm he breathes in.

“So can you.”

He touches the place on my shoulder where the dress strap has left a mark. I am suddenly shy of the unchanged body I have brought him. It seems too easy, too much like something retained in its box. Then his hand moves and it is simply my shoulder.

We get under the cover because the room is cool. There is pleasure, then a difficulty we cannot solve by wanting it harder, then pleasure of a different kind. He starts to say something and I put a finger on his mouth.

“Not an apology.”

He kisses the finger. “I was going to say my arm's trapped.”

We release his arm. Afterwards we lie with the cover pushed down between us and the lamp still on. He has a little pulse at the side of his neck which I can see when he turns his head. I touch it once. He takes my hand and puts it against his chest, where he can hold it without bending his wrist.

“Warm enough?”

“Yes.”

He switches off the lamp. The window has a pale border where the curtains don't meet the wall. A car passes outside and briefly moves that border across the ceiling.

I listen to him settling. A breath held, released; the small drag of a foot against the sheet. He has always needed to arrange his feet before sleep. I had forgotten. I wait for him to do it again, but he is comfortable now.
