# 10. Ellen

We are closing the shed when Leo's phone sounds. He reads the screen with the keys still in his other hand.

“Ellen's at the lane.”

“Here?”

“She asked if she could catch us before we went back.”

He holds the screen towards me, but I am already looking past him. A car comes slowly along the track, stopping once beside a bush before finding the harder ground. The driver has one arm stretched across the passenger seat to hold something still.

My sister gets out carrying a cardboard box.

For a moment I think she has brought a cake to the wrong place. Then she sees me and puts the box back in the car with such care that I want to tell her it can fall over, nobody will mind. She closes the door, opens it again, takes her handbag and closes it properly.

“Look at you,” she says.

I have been looked at all day. From Ellen it feels different, more like being found with something I was not meant to have.

“You've cut your hair.”

“So have several people.”

She comes up to me. I put my arms round her and feel the bones high in her back. Her cheek is warm and damp. She smells of lemon, hand cream and the heated inside of a car. I shut my eyes.

“Oh, you stupid thing,” she says, softly enough for only me.

“I know.”

She gives a little shake of her head against mine. Neither of us has specified what we mean.

When we let go she keeps hold of my wrists. Her face is fuller beneath the eyes, the skin at her neck loose above the collar. The hair is mostly grey, cut to the jaw. I recognise the parting before I recognise the colour. It has resisted our mother's comb, the weather and Ellen's own decisions for as long as I have known her.

“Did you have to do all this this morning?” she asks, indicating the clothes.

“Leo brought them.”

“He's done all right.”

“He's here,” Leo says.

“Yes, I saw you.” She reaches for his arm and squeezes it. “I told Jo I'd come straight over after I'd dropped something off. Then I changed my mind. She won't need me yet.”

He says he will put the last things away, and opens the shed again. Ellen watches him find the right key.

“How is it?” she asks.

I look towards the river. There is a yellow leaf caught against the edge of the landing, turning whenever the water lifts it.

“I've chipped a table.”

“Already?”

“She bought it this morning.”

“She said.”

“And had tea in several places.”

“That sounds like you.”

I pull my wrists gently free. It does sound like me, but I do not want the whole day put away under that heading.

Ellen notices the movement and takes a tissue from her bag. She folds it into a point and wipes underneath one eye, where a little dark line has gathered.

“There's nowhere sensible to sit,” she says.

“There is.”

I lead her to a low bench just outside the shed. The wood is dry except at one end. We sit close together, leaving that end unused. Her handbag presses into my thigh. She moves it to the other side without looking.

For a while we examine our hands. Not openly, not as a planned activity. She looks at the thin red mark the thread has left on my finger and I look at the small brown spots beside her wedding ring. There is an extra ring with a square blue stone. I turn her hand over to see it.

“Yours?”

“No, I mugged somebody on the way.”

“I mean—”

“I bought it. Last year. I went in to have the clasp on a necklace fixed and came out with that.”

“Do you like it?”

“Mostly. It catches on things.”

I run a finger over the stone. I remember holding this hand in a chemist while she had her ears pierced. I was convinced that watching would be worse than having it done. She had told me not to look, then complained that I was not paying attention.

“You went green,” I say.

“At the price?”

“When they did your ears.”

She turns to me. “That was you.”

“No. I held your hand.”

“You were holding it so hard they asked whether you needed a chair.”

I can see the room: the glass shelves, the woman with a white band round one wrist, Ellen's hair pushed behind her ear. In the memory the face which goes pale is hers. I try moving the paleness to my own face and find the rest of the room continues without difficulty.

“You cried afterwards,” I say.

“Because one was higher.”

“It still is.”

She pulls back her hair. The little silver earrings are different shapes, one round, one an irregular drop.

“I've dealt with it.”

I laugh and put my head against her shoulder. She does not move to make it more comfortable. It is a very good shoulder, solid and slightly too high.

Inside, something rolls across the floor. Leo says a word we both know, followed by a smaller one. Ellen and I sit up at the same time.

“Still,” she says.

“Apparently.”

I want to ask her everything. Who is alive, who is married, who has become difficult, whether the cousins still visit each other and what happened to the woman who lived opposite her. There are people I have thought of as occupied somewhere just beyond the day. One question would bring them into it and use up a piece of the afternoon.

Instead I ask about the box.

“Lemon cake. Jo said I could bring something if I didn't make a project of it.”

“Have you?”

“It's one cake.”

“What sort of icing?”

“That's not a project.”

“What sort?”

“A little butter in it. And the lemon.”

“With the layer in the middle?”

“Yes.”

I touch her arm. “I'd have done the same.”

“I know. That's why I didn't ask you.”

She smiles, but her fingers close briefly on the tissue. I think of her mixing it this morning, of how she would have had to choose a bowl, wash a spoon, wait for it to cool, all while I was trying to open a dress. There has been more than one morning today.

“Were you going to come to the place?” I ask.

“Leo wanted to go.”

“That wasn't what I asked.”

“I know.” She smooths the tissue across her knee. “I thought I might. Then I thought of us all standing there.”

I can picture us, lined up with bags and expectations. It would have made a terrible welcome and I still want it, briefly: the evidence of everybody having left something else to come.

“Jo didn't want to,” I say.

Ellen looks towards the water. “She wanted her table.”

I wait for her to make a joke, or to tell me what Jo really wanted. She does neither.

“Did you know she only offered half an hour?”

“Yes.”

“Did you talk about it?”

“We talk.”

It is not a rebuke. It is worse, in some ways, being a plain fact. My sister and my daughter have conversations for which I am not required, even when the subject is me.

“I could ask you to tell me,” I say.

“You could.”

“And you wouldn't?”

“Not everything.”

A fly lands on the blue stone. Ellen lifts her hand and it moves to my trousers. I brush it off more sharply than necessary.

“I'm not trying to catch anybody out.”

“I didn't say you were.”

“No.”

She leans forward to look at the turn-up above my shoe.

“You're going to stand on those.”

“They were bought at a distance.”

“Come here.”

I put one heel forward. She rolls the hem once, then again, folding the seam flat with her thumbnail. It takes her longer than it used to, though I cannot tell whether that is her fingers or the thickness of the cloth. I could do the other leg myself. I let her finish both.

“There.”

“You've always enjoyed telling me to come here.”

“You usually needed to.”

I look at the neat folds. “They'll come down.”

“Then roll them again.”

The simplest of arrangements. She sits back and takes a sweet out of her handbag, unwrapping it with unnecessary noise. She offers me one. I recognise the yellow wrappers and take two, putting the spare in my trouser pocket. The first tastes exactly as I expect, sharp at the edges and then buttery. This particular continuity is almost too much.

Ellen has retired. She tells me because she is describing a woman she used to work with, then stops halfway through a sentence to say that the woman is still there but she isn't. March was her last month. She has not decided what to do with Wednesdays, when she was always busiest. So far she has cleaned a cupboard, gone to the dentist and accidentally booked two lunches for the same day.

“Which did you go to?”

“Neither. By the time I realised, I'd been avoiding both calls.”

This is such an Ellen solution that I start laughing before she reaches the end. She laughs too, one hand over her mouth, the sweet moving against her cheek. Leo comes out and looks at us.

“What?”

“Retirement,” she says.

“Ah.”

He pulls the large door shut. The smaller one waits inside it, still open. Ellen stands and stretches her back, making a face when she thinks I am looking elsewhere.

“Are you sore?” I ask.

“I've been sitting on a plank.”

“Before that.”

“Occasionally.”

I touch the back of her hand. It is not enough time to find out which questions would help and which would simply make her give an account of herself. She turns her palm upwards and catches my fingers.

“I've missed you,” she says.

“I've missed—”

The sentence stops. I do not know how to make it true in the same way. She has had Wednesdays and lemon cakes and days when she could not ring me. I have the memory of being ill and now this bench. I can feel the want of her fiercely without being able to give her fifteen years of having wanted.

She pulls my hand once, sharply enough to spare me having to finish.

“Come on. I'll drive the cake. Leo can drive you.”

For a second I want to ask her to stay. There is room to walk along the bank, and I could ask about the people, one at a time. She would tell me some of it. Jo has not yet had the hour I promised her in the kitchen, and Ellen will be there later with the same handbag and the same uneven earrings.

“What time is it?” I ask Leo.

“Quarter to.”

“Five?”

“Yes.”

I stand, pick up the blue bag and check that the trouser hems have stayed folded. Ellen points to one which has begun to loosen. I bend and roll it again myself.

At her car she lifts the cake box to show me the bottom. A strip of nonslip mat has been cut to fit beneath it. She has thought of the corners on the road and the braking and the distance from her kitchen to Jo's. I tell her this is definitely a project.

“It needs to arrive,” she says.

She puts it back on the seat and draws the belt across it. I wait while she fastens it, then shut her door. Leo is holding mine open. I get in with the bag on my knees, and we follow her out of the lane towards the flat.
