# 3. The turn

There is room for Jo beside me in the back of Leo's car. I move the blue bag to make room, then put it between my feet, where it prevents me from turning towards her. Leo gets out again and opens the boot.

“Give me that.”

“I can hold it.”

“We aren't going to lose the boot.”

I pass it out. Jo pulls the seat belt across herself and waits while I find the part of mine that has slipped under my leg. Her knee touches mine. Neither of us moves it.

Arun has gone on foot with her keys. He says the one-way streets make walking quicker, which Leo disputes without much conviction. The delivery van pulls out before us. I watch its back doors, expecting one to open and the whole purchase to roll into the street.

“It's tied down,” Jo says.

“I know.”

“You keep leaning.”

I sit straight. On the other side of the window Merewick passes in sections I can recognise until the next building arrives. The cinema is still there, though its name has moved onto a board across the front and the little box where they sold tickets has gone. Outside the library, two young trees have become quite unreasonable. Their branches cover the windows of the upstairs room where I once spent three wet afternoons trying to learn accounts.

“They must have to keep the lights on all day now.”

Jo follows my pointing hand.

“In there? It's a dentist.”

“The library?”

“The library moved.”

I turn to look back. A tree gets in the way before I can read the sign. There is no reason to care where people borrow books, but I want to have seen it properly. I am also aware of Jo beside me, available for a question, and cannot bring myself to waste her on the library.

“How long have you lived here?”

“Nearly two years.”

“You like it?”

“Most of it.”

“What's the other part?”

“You'll meet the shower.”

The flat is over a shop with its blinds down. Jo tells Leo to stop at the side entrance. She is out before he has finished putting the car into neutral. Arun is standing in the open doorway, and a narrow wooden shoe rack stands beside him on the pavement.

“We're very welcoming,” he says. “We put the furniture outside for you.”

Jo picks up a boot which has fallen from the rack and sets it next to its partner. They are heavy boots with white dust in the creases. I have not seen her wear them, but know at once that they are hers. The way she puts the left one down, hooking the other with its heel, is the way she used to leave trainers in the middle of our kitchen.

“The radiator's the problem,” Arun tells Leo. “And this.”

He opens the door wide. Its handle comes to rest against a rubber stop before the door reaches the wall.

The man in the purple fleece arrives carrying the detached legs under one arm. His van is parked partly across the side entrance, with its hazard lights flashing against the shop window. He looks at the stairs, then at Jo.

“First floor?”

“I told you.”

“You said the flat.”

“I said the flat up one flight of stairs.”

He takes the receipt when she offers it, though I don't think there can be anything written on it which will prove what either of them said. Leo goes to look at the van.

“I'll take the other end,” he says. “Arun, can you watch the turn?”

“We need to look first,” Jo says.

She puts the table legs against the wall and leads us upstairs. The staircase is wider than the doorway and arrives at a landing with a high window. A rubber plant stands in the corner, leaning away from the glass. Jo carries it into the flat. I follow with a little stool which has been underneath it and find myself in a white hall, facing a radiator with a row of gloves drying along its top.

“Work gloves,” Jo says, taking them off. “I washed them.”

“I haven't said anything.”

“You picked one up with two fingers.”

It is stiff and pale in the palm. I put it with the others on the stool. Jo measures from the radiator to the opposite wall, then opens the door of a cupboard and presses herself into the space beyond it.

“That gives us somewhere to stand. The top can go into the front room before we turn it.”

Arun comes up with the legs. He lays them on an old blanket in the front room and returns for the top. I have a moment alone in Jo's hall. There is a coat on the peg nearest me, brown with a narrow belt, and beneath it a shopping bag full of empty jars. The hall smells faintly of toast. I touch the coat, only the end of one sleeve. It is soft and slightly damp. There is a place at its cuff where the lining has begun to come out, and I push it back inside with my finger.

“Mum?”

I leave the coat and go downstairs.

The table top has become much larger since it was taken out of the warehouse. Upright between Leo and the seller, it reaches above their waists. Its legs are gone, but the shallow framework beneath it catches the light in square corners. I can see why Jo has moved the plant. Nothing about it looks willing to bend.

They come through the outside door slowly, one edge leading. Arun walks backwards ahead of them, watching the wall. Jo stays on the landing. I shut the door against the stop so it cannot swing onto Leo's fingers, then hurry up behind them. The sandwich bag is in my hand. I have carried it all the way from the warehouse as if someone has trusted me with a tiny animal.

At the top of the stairs they stop. Leo rests his end on a folded towel while the seller changes his grip.

“All right?” I ask.

“Yes.”

Leo's face is red. I have the urge to wipe his forehead, a gesture so much like nursing that I put my hand into my pocket instead. The envelope is there. I press it flat against my hip.

“We go in straight,” Jo says. “Then lift over the radiator and turn towards the room.”

They get through her front door. There is enough room, just as the tape measure said, though the reality of two men, a table and their hands is less reassuring than a strip of numbers. Arun goes into the cupboard space. I stand on the other side of the hall where Jo tells me, ready to move the blanket under the edge when they put it down.

“This end up,” the seller says.

Leo lifts. The top rises. Its lower edge is going to strike the freshly painted doorframe. I can see the small white ridge at the corner, the thick part of the paint where someone has taken care. I put the folded blanket between it and the oak, and the blanket slips. I catch the edge of the table with both hands and push it away from the frame.

There is a crack, not loud, and a little piece of wood falls onto my shoe.

“Stop,” Jo says.

Everybody stops. For one moment the table hangs there, held by people who cannot see the part nearest them.

“Down,” Leo says. “We need to put it down.”

They lower it onto the blanket. I step back. The little piece of wood stays on my shoe until I bend to pick it up. It is the size of the end of my thumb, pale on one side and polished brown on the other.

The metal catch on the inside of the cupboard has a pale mark across it.

“I was keeping it off the door,” I say.

Jo crouches beside the table. A shallow bite has gone out of the rim. It isn't the split she found at the warehouse; that lies underneath, on the far side. This is new and bright, where anyone sitting down will see it.

“I moved it,” I say.

“I saw.”

She stands. Her mouth has gone narrow. The seller looks at the catch, at my hands, and then deliberately at his watch.

“We still need to get it in,” she says.

Arun holds the cupboard door completely back. I move out of the hall. They lift the top again, this time far enough above the radiator to turn it gently towards the open front room. I watch from the kitchen doorway. Nobody asks for my help. The table passes into the room and is lowered flat onto the blanket, and the seller straightens with both hands against his back.

“There.”

Jo goes to get the legs. Leo thanks the man and helps him take the towels downstairs. The hazard lights stop flickering across the ceiling. I am left beside the new table, holding the bit I have broken off it.

“We could glue it.”

Jo puts down the first leg. “Not now.”

“If we keep the piece.”

“Yes, keep it.”

I put it in the sandwich bag, then remember her instruction about nothing else going in there. I take it out again. Arun, coming back through the hall, watches me do this without comment.

“Have you got an envelope?” I ask.

Jo points to a bowl on a shelf. It contains letters, a pencil sharpener and a small green stone. I take an opened gas bill and fold the piece of wood into an empty corner. On the back I write TABLE in Jo's pencil. The pencil has followed me from the warehouse. I have put it in my pocket without asking, as easily as breathing.

“Sorry,” I say, handing it to her.

“You can use it.”

“I mean about the table.”

“I know.”

We assemble it on the blanket. There is a little work to be done by each of us, even me. I empty the bolts and washers onto a saucer and find the number one I darkened earlier. Leo holds the corresponding leg while Jo fastens it. Arun reads out the other numbers as though calling a dance. We do not laugh as much as we did in the warehouse, but we do laugh once when Leo finds his glasses beneath the blanket and accuses the table of standing on them before it has been stood up.

At last all four of us turn it over. The round top arrives at its proper height. Jo takes two steps away and looks at it.

I have broken it before she has eaten a meal at it. The thought is so complete that I cannot find anything to add. She comes back and puts her hand flat on the surface. Then her other hand. She leans.

One foot ticks against the floor.

“It's the boards,” Arun says.

“Let me see.”

She moves the table a little. The tick moves with it, changing sides. Leo gets down to look at the feet. He has a knee on the blanket and one trouser leg pulled up enough to show a pale strip of skin above his sock.

“I've got some felt pads,” he says. “At mine. A thin one will do it.”

“A folded bit of paper will do it for now,” Jo says.

She tears a strip from the gas bill, well away from my folded parcel. The table steadies. She presses again, harder. The feet stay still.

“There,” she says.

Arun sets an imaginary plate in front of himself and leans on both elbows. “Luxury.”

Jo sits in the chair beside him. The new chip is by her left hand. She runs a finger near it without touching the sharp part.

“We'll smooth that before anyone catches a sleeve,” she says. “Dad, haven't you got some of that fine sandpaper?”

“Yes.”

“Could you bring it?”

He gets up slowly, pushing on his thigh. “I'll go now. Won't be long.”

I look from him to Jo. He is going somewhere without me. Jo is sitting at her table without appearing to wish me gone. For a moment both facts require more thought than I can give them.

“Stay,” he says quietly, passing me. “I'll come back.”

I nod. His hand touches my elbow, no more than the touch in Rose's doorway. Then he goes down the stairs with the blanket over his arm, leaving the four legs firmly underneath the table and me beside my daughter, where I had wanted to be.
