# 4. Upstairs

Jo takes off her jacket and hangs it over a chair. Underneath she has a dark blue shirt with a small white line drawn on one cuff. I rub my finger over it before I have thought to ask.

“Chalk,” she says.

“It'll wash out.”

“That's the intention.”

Arun is in the kitchen, turning on the kettle. He knows which cupboard holds the mugs and where she keeps the tea, but has to open three drawers to find a spoon. I watch him as he moves. I cannot tell whether his ease means anything except that he has been here before.

“Milk?” he asks.

“Please.”

“Sugar?”

“No.”

He puts the milk back. Jo looks around the room with both hands on her hips, considering the next thing. I know the stance, though not from her. I have seen myself stand like that in photographs of holidays, deciding where our possessions ought to go.

“I need to clear the old table,” she says.

Against the window a narrow folding table is entirely covered. There are papers, a laptop, two strips of painted wood and a pair of orange-handled scissors. A cutting mat shows through in one corner. Beneath it, on the floor, are three boxes and a roll of something grey. No one could have put a plate down there without first becoming involved in her work.

“Where does it all go?” I ask.

“Usually on the floor, then back here.”

“An established system.”

“I'm trying to advance it.”

She opens one of the boxes. There are folders inside, upright and surprisingly orderly. She takes out a green one and hands me a stack of drawings.

“These can go in here. Face up, same way round. The ones with a cross through them are old. Keep them underneath.”

“Old but not unwanted?”

“Exactly.”

I sit by the window. The chair has a cushion so thin it may once have been folded from a towel. Jo moves the two strips of wood to the new table, putting newspaper beneath them. She carries them carefully, as though they belong to someone who will notice damage. One has a little ridge of green paint along an edge. The other is bare and scarred with small nail holes.

“From a building?” I ask.

“Window mouldings. Samples. We had them made to compare with an old section.”

“You designed them?”

“I drew them. Someone else made them.”

“They look good.”

“One's wrong.”

She turns the bare one against the light, showing me a shallow curve. Her fingernail follows it. I can see the difference when the two are together, though separately I would have sworn they were the same.

“Can they do it again?”

“Yes. If I tell them what I mean this time.”

Arun brings the tea. He moves a drawing away from the place he chooses for my mug, checks that there is nothing underneath it and puts the mug down. He does this without asking, and Jo lets him.

“I thought you said it was their mistake,” he says.

“I said it might be.”

“It was very much their mistake yesterday.”

She looks at him over the little strip of wood. “Yesterday I hadn't opened the file I'd actually sent.”

“Ah.”

“You can stop there.”

He grins and goes back to the kitchen. A cupboard closes. The kettle clicks off again; he has turned it on for himself after forgetting to fill his mug.

I start putting the drawings into the folder. They are elevations, sections and things I can follow only when I turn the page sideways. Fine lines end in arrows with numbers between them. Jo's name is printed in a small box at the bottom, alongside the practice's name and an address. Seeing it there is unexpectedly disturbing. Nobody has written Josephine, which I still think of as the name to use when something needs to be official. Jo is sufficient to be paid for work.

“Do you like it?” I ask.

“Which?”

“What you do.”

She holds up the wrong moulding. “Today?”

“Most days.”

“Yes.”

I wait, and she looks down at the wood again.

“I like going into places before anything's been decided. Sometimes you can still see how something was put together. The marks from the tools. The bits someone changed because the wall wasn't straight.”

She places the two pieces beside each other.

“I don't like arguing over invoices.”

“Nobody likes that.”

“Some people do. I've met them.”

On the shelf above the folding table is my cake tin. I notice it while looking for somewhere to put the finished folder. Green sides, a silver lid, a printed bunch of flowers with one petal nearly rubbed away. It used to hold the cake I made for Sunday, or half a loaf when I had not made one. There is a dent near the catch from a time I dropped it with nothing inside. I can see the dent even from here.

“You've got it.”

Jo turns.

“That tin.”

“Yes.”

I stand and reach for it. There is weight in it, enough that I use both hands to bring it down. For a moment I don't open it. I imagine small things from my life arranged inside, the objects too slight for anybody else to bother with. A brooch. The photograph from my driving licence. One of the notes I left on the kitchen table telling her where I had gone.

The lid sticks, just as it used to. I press the catch with my thumbnail and raise it.

Cables. Thick black ones, thin white ones, a little plastic box with two slots in it. One of the cables springs up and catches the lid before it can fall shut.

“The short grey one isn't mine,” Jo says. “I need to take it back on Monday.”

“You keep these in it.”

“Otherwise they get everywhere.”

I put a finger into the space beside them. The inside still has the scratches left by a bread knife. I lift the longest cable, hoping for something beneath it, and find a plug.

Jo comes over. She frees the lid from the cable and closes it properly.

“It's a good tin.”

“Yes.”

“Did you want—”

“No.”

I say it too quickly. She leaves the tin beside the folder.

“This one has a cross on the back,” I say, picking up a drawing. “Is that old too?”

She comes to look. We put it under the others.

There is a bedroom off the hall and a bathroom beyond it. I ask where I can wash my hands. Jo opens the bathroom door, then reaches past me to remove a pair of tights from the basin.

“Sorry.”

“I used to own a sink.”

“There's a clean towel on the rail. The tap goes further than you think.”

It does. Water hits the basin and comes back at my dress. I turn it down, laughing once to myself, and wash the dust from my fingers. In the mirror I look almost as I did in the changing room. A red patch shows above my collar where I have been carrying the table. I pull the green fabric away from it and see that the stitching around the pocket is loose in one place, where Jo's zip caught it.

Beside the mirror are three postcards tucked into the frame. A dark blue bowl. A building with a pink front. A drawing of a dog wearing a cone. The last one has HAPPY MOVING printed underneath in tiny letters. I look at it for a long time without learning anything useful. Through the wall I can hear Jo and Arun discussing whether his car is still at the restaurant. He says it is. She says he ought to have brought it. He says that would not have widened her hallway.

I close the door completely and sit on the edge of the bath. It is the first place I have sat today where nobody can see whether I am having a nice time. A towel hangs beside my face, warm from the rail. I press it against the sore place under my collar, not rubbing, and listen to the water settling in the pipes. On the floor a little brush has fallen behind the waste bin. I pick it up, clean a ring of dust from its handle with my thumb and put it on the shelf. Then I take the envelope out of my pocket and check that it hasn't bent while I was carrying the table. The corners of the photographs are firm beneath the paper. I put it back without opening it and stay another minute, until I want to go out.

When I come out, Jo is kneeling on the floor looking for a piece of paper beneath the folding table. I kneel beside her and find a pen, two hair clips and a receipt for something which has faded out of existence.

“What colour?”

“White. Little. It has a number on it.”

“We've narrowed it down.”

She reaches further, her cheek almost against the floor. I see the pale patch at the back of her neck again. When she was little I could put a whole hand there and steer her away from something dangerous or merely inconvenient. Now the hand would belong in no sensible part of what she is doing. I use it to lift a box.

Underneath is a white slip with a telephone number written on it. I give it to her.

“Thank you.”

She stays on her knees while she enters the number into her phone. Then she calls, standing halfway through the first ring. I retreat to my folder, hearing only her side of the conversation. She says she's checked, that the mistake was in the earlier drawing, that she'll send the corrected one now. She listens with her head slightly tilted. Her free hand is rubbing a pencil mark from the back of the other. It doesn't come off.

“No, I understand. Monday's fine.”

She listens again.

“Yes. Put that against my time. Thank you.”

When she ends the call, Arun holds out her tea. She takes it and drinks, looking at the screen of the laptop.

“Sorted?”

“Expensively.”

“They won't be there forever,” I say.

She looks up.

“The wrong ones. Once they're replaced.”

“They haven't been fitted, Mum.”

“Good.”

I have misunderstood so thoroughly that she smiles. It is a tired smile, but I take it. She sits, finds the right drawing and begins again.

Arun takes the empty mugs to the kitchen. I follow him with mine.

“I said I'd collect a dish,” he says quietly. “From work. She's forgotten.”

“I haven't,” Jo calls from the other room.

“She has extraordinarily good hearing when she's working,” he tells me.

“I made that.”

I hear myself. Arun looks at me, and I cannot quite tell whether he thinks I mean her hearing, or Jo, or the whole room we are standing in. I rinse my mug before he can answer.

Jo appears in the doorway with a pad of paper.

“Could you get a few things while you're out?” she asks him. “I was going to go, but I need to finish this.”

“Give me the list.”

She writes, tears off the page, then looks at me.

“Would you like to go with him?”

I would like to stay. I would also like her to finish quickly so she will stop looking at that screen. Outside the window the light has changed; a patch of sun lies on the table near the chip, making it look much smaller than it felt in my hand.

“Yes,” I say. “If Arun doesn't mind.”

“Arun's grateful for another pair of hands.”

Jo gives him a shopping bag, then gives me a second one. Mine has a loose handle which she knots once before letting go.

“Don't put the heavy things in that.”

“What counts as heavy?”

She takes the list back and adds something at the bottom. “You can use your judgement.”

It is an ordinary thing to say. She has said it while writing, without looking up, and I hear a voice from before she learned to speak properly, telling me very firmly that she could do her coat by herself. I almost tell her. Instead I take the bag and put my hand through the loop she has made.
