# 8. A smaller kitchen

Leo parks beside a hedge which has grown into the space meant for the passenger. I get out by bringing both knees round together. A twig drags along my calf and releases me with a small elastic movement. He has come halfway round to help and stops when he sees me standing.

Leo opens the boot and brings out the blue clothes bag. I take it from him before we go up the path. The cottage has two steps and a green door, darker than my dress. A piece of cardboard fills the bottom pane of a window in the adjoining shed. I can see the tooth of a saw through the pane above it. Here, where I have never lived, he puts the key straight into the lock. There is no searching.

“Mind the—”

I step over the raised sill.

“You're going to run out of those,” I say.

“Things to mind?”

“Eventually.”

He puts his keys on a hook. The house smells of coffee and a cloth that has been left damp. It is not unpleasant. I am relieved it is not a smell I recognise from a hospital, then irritated to have considered that a possibility simply because he is older.

The kitchen is immediately on the left. Two people can stand in it if one of them is willing to be in the way. A draining board folds down over the sink. Leo lifts it to rinse the kettle and some spoons slide towards his wrist. He catches them without looking. I wait for him to find places for my presence.

“Bathroom's through there. The door sticks at the bottom.”

“Is that where you imagined I'd want to change?”

“Unless you'd rather—”

“No. It's good.”

I say it too quickly, as if he has offered me somewhere to live. He looks towards the little room behind the kitchen. There is a sofa with a blanket folded over one arm, a television and a tall lamp. A book lies face down on the floor beside the sofa. I cannot read its title from here. That seems an avoidable failure of intimacy.

“Do you like it?” he asks.

“The cottage?”

He nods.

I want to give a generous answer which will also leave him in no doubt about what he has done. We have already stood at the other gate. He has watched me watch a woman handle a garden that used to be ours. It would be easy to make him do the same thing again in this kitchen.

“I like that you can catch the spoons.”

“That took a while.”

He puts water on. I take the blue bag into the bathroom. I put it down and wash my hands, working the soil out from beside the nails. The last dark streak circles the drain and goes.

The mirror is narrow, and close enough to inspect myself whether I want to or not. I look like somebody who has been carrying furniture. There is dust on the green fabric at my hip and a bright patch on my cheek where I have rubbed it. I raise my arm and see the small hollow beneath it, familiar skin assembled into an unfamiliar sight after a day of looking so hard at other people.

The dress catches at the waist. I get the zip down by stretching one shoulder until it hurts. This morning there was a woman to help. I could call Leo. Instead I pull my arm through the neck, and for a moment the dress is round my head and the world smells of my own warm hair.

When it is off I hold it up. The yellow stitching has pulled where Jo's jacket caught. There is a loose loop. I tuck it into the pocket and feel paper.

The photographs.

I draw out the envelope before folding the dress. Its flap has bent where Leo and I held opposite ends at breakfast. A grey crescent of dust marks one corner. All morning I have been carrying evidence of things I have not asked to see.

I lay it on the closed toilet lid while I put on the trousers. They fit at the hips and are long in the leg. The shirt smells of a cupboard, the clean smell that belongs to cloth waiting. I fasten the buttons from the bottom because the top one is too near my face to inspect. In this shirt I can see more clearly that I am forty-one. The dress has made a costume of me. These clothes make an afternoon.

The socks are still joined at the toes.

“Leo?”

He answers from very near the door.

“Scissors?”

There is a silence, then a drawer being opened. He hands them round the door without coming in. The blades are green-handled and too large for the job. I cut the little plastic link and hold both pieces in my palm so they will not end up in somebody's foot.

“Thank you.”

“I've got the small ones somewhere.”

“These worked.”

“Yes.”

I put the original shoes beside the folded dress in the bag. The envelope fits in an inner pocket with a zip which has been left open. I do not zip it yet.

When I come out Leo is leaning against the worktop with his tea. He looks at the shirt, then at my face. His eyes move back to the shirt.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“You bought it.”

“I know.”

I stand between his knees. His cup is inconveniently placed, so he reaches behind himself and puts it down without checking for space. I have to move a tin before it lands. Then his hands are at my waist, and the shirt, which a moment ago was useful, is something he can feel me through.

I kiss him. We do it properly this time. His mouth is familiar in the middle and different at the edges. He draws breath against my cheek. I had forgotten the effort of kissing somebody who is trying not to tremble.

The kettle clicks again. He has put it back on without meaning to.

“That's going to be very boiled,” I say.

He rests his forehead against mine. I feel the pressure of it, and for several seconds I let the water matter to somebody else.

We take the cups into the small sitting room. He picks up the book from the floor and uses it to press down a corner of the rug. I sit on the sofa, leaving him room. He chooses the chair opposite. I cannot tell whether this is deliberate or simply where he usually sits, and I am not yet able to tolerate the second possibility more easily than the first.

I bring out the envelope.

“Shall we?”

He puts his cup down.

The boat is on top. I lay it on my knee. Below it is Jo in a coat with a bright strip across the chest, holding a piece of something pale. I begin to ask what it is, but a face has appeared on the edge of the next photograph. A woman stands beside Leo on a beach. Rain or spray has darkened the front of her trousers. Her hair is flattened across one eye, and her free hand is inside his open coat.

I slide the picture out. He waits.

“Who's this?”

“Catherine.”

I look again. She is not young. I am ashamed of the speed with which I look for that, and of the relief that follows. Her hand is inside his coat because she has hold of the dry part of his jumper. I know how the hem will feel bunched in her fist.

“We were together,” he says.

“For how long?”

“About six years.”

A length of time large enough to contain separate winters, bills, mornings when neither of them wants to get up. I had been prepared for someone. I had not prepared a sensible size for her.

“And now?”

“We stopped seeing each other two years ago.”

I place the picture on the sofa between us, though he is not there.

“You brought this to breakfast.”

“Yes.”

“Out of six.”

“Yes.”

I look at the other pictures in my hand as if their number could still change.

“I wanted to tell you,” he says. “I thought if I put it in, I couldn't get to the end of the day and find I hadn't.”

“So it was for you.”

“Some of it.”

He is not apologising quickly enough. The thought arrives so plainly that I have to put down the rest of the photographs.

“Did she live here?”

“She had her own house. She stayed.”

“Where does she live now?”

“Near her son.”

“Did you have to leave each other for that?”

He rubs his thumb along the arm of the chair. There is a worn patch which his thumb finds without assistance.

“No. That came afterwards.”

I wait for a reason small enough to understand and satisfactory enough to punish nobody. He does not produce it.

“She wanted us to make some decisions,” he says eventually. “I kept thinking we could keep things as they were until we agreed. We didn't agree.”

“About living together?”

“Among other things.”

“And you were happy?”

“Yes.”

He says it before I have quite finished. Then he looks at me as if he expects the word to have struck something visible.

I pick up my tea. The handle is turned away and I have to move the whole cup. I do this slowly. He has not waited fifteen years to tell me that he was happy with another woman. He has waited less than a day. The distinction does nothing useful in my stomach.

“Did Jo know her?”

“Yes.”

“Of course she did.”

“She didn't have to keep it from you, Flora.”

“I know that.”

He starts to say something else and stops. I want him to continue, so that I can reject it rather than have to sit with the thing I have said.

On the beach Catherine's shoes are dangling from two fingers. One has a strap with a buckle. Her bare feet are pale and quite ugly, broad across the toes. I find myself liking them. It feels disloyal to something, though I cannot decide which of us has the better claim.

“Where is it?” I ask.

“The photograph? Norfolk.”

“Was the sea warm?”

“No. We went in anyway. She said it couldn't be worse once we were wet.”

“And was it?”

“Much.”

I laugh. The laugh is a real one, brief and unwelcome. Leo leans back in his chair. There is a way the cushion receives him which I have never seen.

“I don't know what you want me to say.”

“You can say what you want.”

“That isn't the same.”

“No.”

I turn the picture over. There is a year and a place in his larger handwriting. Nothing on the back explains how to hold it.

“I want to be allowed to dislike it for a minute,” I say.

“All right.”

“Without that meaning I wish you hadn't.”

He looks towards the window. “I don't think you have to be very fair about it immediately.”

I would like to thank him for this and also point out that he has made an absurdly generous offer on my behalf. Instead I put the photograph back beneath Jo's picture. Catherine is still there. The order has not solved anything, but I can breathe without looking at her hand.

Leo takes his cup to the kitchen for more hot water. On the way he catches the rug with his heel, dislodging the book. He bends to straighten it, makes it worse and finally leaves it. That is familiar enough to hurt in a different place.

“Do you still put the hot-water bottle against your feet?” I ask when he comes back.

“In winter.”

“Never the other end?”

“No.”

“You used to put it on my side first.”

“I remember.”

The memory has come out like a demand. I look down at my shoes. The new soles are soft enough to bend when I press my toes against the floor.

“I liked that,” I say.

He comes and sits beside me. The sofa slopes towards his usual end. Our knees touch, and he moves his cup to the floor before putting his hand over mine.

For a while we drink what is left of the tea. A child shouts in a garden beyond the hedge, calling the same name until another child answers with such fury that Leo smiles. He tells me which garden has the trampoline. It is a trivial piece of information he can give without taking anything away.

I ask him about the book. It is a history of a railway line he has never travelled on. He has reached the part about the foundations of a bridge, and he tells me more than I need to know about the expense of sinking them into mud. I listen until his voice has stopped being careful.

Then I ask to see the garden.

It is scarcely wider than the kitchen, with pots against the fence and a patch of paving where a chair has stood long enough to keep the weeds down. He shows me a tomato plant barely taller than my hand. A second pot contains only a stick.

“What happened there?”

“We don't discuss that one.”

I touch the warm rim of the empty pot. Through the hedge comes the sound of the children negotiating a rule neither intends to observe. I would like to sit down here with my feet against the paving and let the day get longer without asking it to.

Leo looks at his watch.

“The shed?” he asks.

“Yes.”

I go in for the bag. Before closing its inner pocket, I check that all six photographs are inside the envelope. I leave them in his order. Then I zip it shut and lift the bag by both handles, with the green dress out of sight.
