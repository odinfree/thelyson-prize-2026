# 21. On the bank

Jo has brought two flasks and only one cup. She discovers this while unpacking them on the bench beside the landing.

“The lids?” I say.

She holds one up. It is a complicated screw thing with a spout in it.

“That's taking progress too far,” I say.

“We'll manage.”

She pours something into the cup and gives it to me. Coffee. I drink before asking whether there is sugar in it, and find that there is none. It is bitter and very hot. I keep both hands around the cup.

“You came.”

“Yes.”

I want to say more, to make her understand that I have noticed her leaving her bed and coming out into this cold ordinary morning. She is already taking the second flask to Leo. He has tied the boat and is looking at the angle of the trolley.

“Tea,” she says.

“Thank you.”

“One cup. You'll have to wait.”

He glances at his watch. “We've got time.”

I look too. Five to six. There is time for tea to cool and for the boat to be put away. I drink again because my hands want the warmth and my mouth doesn't know how to wait for it.

Jo stands beside me. Her hair has a flat place at the back where she slept on it. The rest has been pushed behind her ears. Under the dark coat she is wearing trousers and a jumper with a loose thread at the cuff.

“Did you sleep?” I ask.

“Some.”

“I'm sorry.”

“About sleeping?”

“About making it so early.”

She looks at the river. “It couldn't be much later.”

“No.”

I give her the cup. She takes a careful mouthful and makes the same face I did. Then she drinks some more.

“How was it?”

“I was very good.”

“She's had one lesson,” Leo says.

“An intensive course.”

Jo looks at my damp trouser leg. I spread my hands, unable to defend it while she can see the evidence. She smiles and comes close enough for me to put an arm around her. Her shoulder is solid beneath the coat. We stand side by side rather than facing each other, which lets me keep looking without examining her face for instructions.

Leo clears his throat. “Shall we get this out before it drifts under the boards?”

“It's tied,” Jo says.

“The stern isn't.”

She hands him the cup and goes to the trolley. He takes one mouthful, realises it is coffee and looks accusingly at it.

“We said there was only one,” she tells him.

“I didn't know you were both drinking coffee.”

“You could have asked.”

I take the cup from him. We finish it between us while Jo positions the trolley. She has done this before. I can see it in the way she puts one foot against the wheel to keep it from rolling, then checks where the handles will be when the weight comes onto it.

“Like this?” I ask.

She shows me where to hold. Her hand touches my wrist, moving it a little farther from the point that will meet the support. The instruction is quick and impersonal. I like receiving it.

We get the boat out in stages. Leo guides, Jo takes the weight where she can and I keep the other end from swinging. It is heavier now than it seemed in the shed. Or I am tired. Probably both. We stop halfway up the slope and rest the trolley while he shifts his grip.

“All right?” Jo asks him.

“Yes.”

She keeps watching. “Knee?”

He looks at me as if I have organised this.

“Everyone keeps asking.”

“Because you keep doing that with it.”

He straightens the leg. I recognise last night's movement. Jo waits until he has done it twice, then takes the position he was using and tells him to go at the lighter end. He starts to object, thinks better of it or grows tired of objecting, and moves.

At the shed she opens the wide door while we hold the boat still. The inside is lit with a band of morning sun. Dust moves in it where our sleeves pass.

Putting the boat on its stands is more difficult than taking it off. I want to lower my end before the stand is underneath it. Leo tells me to wait. My arms begin to shake. Jo gets the pad into place and we lower it together.

For a few seconds nobody speaks. I rest my palms against my thighs and look at the boat. It is back where it belongs, with a little river water under the seat and a leaf stuck to the blue paint.

“There,” Leo says.

Jo picks the leaf off and holds it out to me.

“Souvenir.”

I take it. It is damaged down one side and has a very small snail on the other.

“This one's occupied.”

She leans in to look. I carry it back outside and put it on the ground by the wall, where there are other leaves and a little dampness. When I return, they are taking the oars out of the boat.

We wipe them with cloths. The wet wood darkens the fabric. I give particular attention to the leather I stitched until Leo says that will do, and I hand the oar over. He puts it on its brackets. Jo is wiping the other, her sleeves pushed up above the wrists.

“I got your message,” she says.

“Which?”

“The flat.”

He keeps a hand on the oar, settling it in place. “Oh. Yes. You needn't do anything about it.”

“I wasn't going to.”

“I just thought it looked worth seeing.”

“I'm not looking.”

“You said the heating—”

“I said the landlord needs to fix it.”

He takes the other oar from her. “It was only a link, Jo.”

She folds her wet cloth in half. “It's never only one.”

I look at the boat's little pool of water. There is a sponge in a bucket nearby. I take it and begin to soak up the water, pressing it firmly enough to keep my attention in my hand.

“I don't understand what I'm meant to do,” Leo says.

“You don't always have to do something.”

“If you tell me there's a problem—”

“Sometimes I'm just telling you.”

I could explain her to him. I could explain him to her. I have theories enough for both, particularly now I have listened to one private call and one conversation in a shed. The sponge is full. I squeeze it into the bucket and go back for the rest.

They do not finish the argument. Jo takes off her coat because she is warm from lifting, then puts it on again because the shed is cold. Leo hangs his buoyancy aid and reaches for mine, forgetting it is still around me. I undo it and hand it over; we both make a little embarrassed movement, reaching too soon. He hangs it beside his and checks that the fastening won't knock against the wall.

“I'll ring about the heating on Monday,” Jo says eventually.

“I can give you—”

She looks at him.

He stops. “All right.”

She doesn't thank him for stopping. He doesn't look satisfied. We put the cloths where they can dry and leave the matter among other things that will still exist on Monday.

Outside, the tea has cooled in the flask. Leo drinks it from the cup, then pours more and gives it to me. Jo opens a paper bag and takes out three pieces of lemon cake wrapped in the same paper they were brought in last night.

“Breakfast,” she says.

“We've had eggs.”

“Then cake.”

Mine has a thick end of icing. I eat that part first. A crumb sticks to the canvas jacket and I brush it off before it can work its way into the fold of the cuff.

“Ellen asked me to say goodbye,” Jo says.

I stop chewing. “How is she?”

“Tired. She messaged when I was leaving. Said she wouldn't come and make everybody cry at a rowing club.”

“She'd manage without the club.”

Jo smiles. “She said you'd say something like that.”

I look down at the cake, which my sister made or brought or arranged for my daughter's birthday while I was still a proposal in the morning. I take another bite. The lemon catches at the sides of my tongue.

“Will you tell her I liked it?”

“You told her yesterday.”

“Again.”

“All right.”

After we have eaten, Jo says she wants a photograph. She doesn't say we ought to have one or that we will regret not having it. She wants one. Leo takes his glasses off, then puts them back on when she asks why he has done that.

“They reflect.”

“You wear glasses.”

“I know that.”

She props her phone against her folded gloves on the car bonnet. Leo has unlocked the car to put the flask away; I can see the blue bag on the back seat through the open door. Jo checks the picture on the screen, moves the phone a little and comes to stand between us.

There is a small ticking sound. I feel rushed by it and look at her instead of the phone. She says, “Mum,” and the photograph is taken while I am turning.

We look at the result together. Leo is squinting. Jo has one eye partly closed. I look as if somebody has called my name, which they have.

“Again,” Leo says.

“I like it,” Jo says.

“You would. You've only lost an eye.”

She laughs and takes another, holding the phone herself this time so we are all too close to it. I can see our faces in the little glass. My face is close beside Jo's, with only eleven years between them. I could be her older sister. I move back a little, which makes no useful difference, and Jo takes the picture before I have arranged anything.

She looks at it for a moment, then puts the phone away.

“Send me that,” Leo says.

“I will.”

There is a finality in the small click of the car door when he shuts it. I don't like it. I walk a few steps towards the water and they follow without asking where I am going.

The bench is dry at one end. We sit where we can, Jo on the dry part, Leo and I making the cold damp discovery together. The river is brighter now. From the far side comes the sound of a door being rolled up, a rattle that takes several seconds to finish.

Jo puts her hand on my knee. I lay mine over it. Her nails are cut short, with a dark edge still under one of them from yesterday's work. I turn her hand to see her palm, then turn it back before she has to ask what I am looking for.

“Does it hurt?” she asks.

“What?”

“Being here.”

I think about the rowing, my forehead, the sore patch in my mouth, the sudden hard fear at Leo's door.

“In places.”

She nods. I don't know which answer she has heard. I keep her hand under mine and look at the bit of water directly in front of us, where something too small to see keeps making a ring and then another.
