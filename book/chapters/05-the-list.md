# 5. The list

Arun holds the outside door for me, then has to catch the empty shoe rack before it falls into the street. We have all forgotten it. He carries it back to the hall, and I line Jo's boots on the bottom shelf. The toes are scuffed almost white. I rub one with my thumb before standing up.

“Ready?”

He gives me the list. Jo's writing slants more when she is in a hurry. I recognise TOMATOES, then something that might be a measurement.

“What are these?”

“Two tins.”

“Ah. Not two tons.”

“We'd need the other bag.”

Outside, the street slopes towards a junction with a painted cycle lane. Arun walks slowly enough that I begin to wonder whether he has been asked to. I speed up. He matches me, still with his hands in his pockets. The list moves against my fingers in the little wind.

“You can go your usual pace,” I say.

“This is my usual pace.”

“Good.”

“Are we late for something?”

“No.”

I slow down. At the corner we both wait for a car which is farther away than I thought. Arun looks at the sky. There is a thin strip of blue over the roofs and a larger cloud coming towards it. It seems strange that he has nothing he urgently needs to ask me.

“How do you know Jo?”

“Through Nina. Nina used to work with my sister.”

“Does everyone know everyone?”

“Eventually.”

He looks for traffic again, then crosses. I follow. On the other side he points down a narrow passage between two shops.

“Dish first. Then food. Unless you want to carry the tomatoes twice.”

I like him better for not offering me a choice he has already worked out. The passage opens onto a yard with bins along one wall and a back door propped open by a crate. The smell changes from street dust to hot oil and something savoury which makes me hungry despite the eggs. A young woman in a black apron comes out holding a tea towel around a bowl.

“There you are.”

“I'm not on yet,” Arun says.

“I know. I need someone with another hand.”

He takes the bowl. She goes back inside and returns with a large oval dish, white with a blue line around the rim. It is heavier than I expect when she passes it to me. A chip beneath one handle has been rubbed smooth by use.

“That's the last big one,” she says. “Tell her I want it washed.”

“She washes things.”

“So you all say.”

She looks at me, waiting to be introduced.

“This is Flora,” Arun says. “Jo's mum. Flora, this is Tessa.”

Tessa's eyes move to my face. She looks at Arun once, very quickly. Then she folds the tea towel over her arm.

“Nice to meet you. Mind that handle; there's a bit underneath.”

“I've found it.”

“Good. Not good. You know what I mean.”

I smile. She smiles back. Arun has taken the dish from me and wrapped it in the towel, leaving her to notice that he now has both things.

“Bring that back as well.”

“Anything else?”

She gives him a look. “One till five. You said.”

“I said one till five.”

“He's asking if you can stay for the evening.”

“I'm going to Jo's.”

“I've told him.”

“Tell him again.”

She nods, not offended, and disappears into the heat. Arun stands for a moment looking at the doorway. The bowl she gave him first is still in his hand.

“Was that meant to go somewhere?” I ask.

“Probably.”

He takes it inside. I wait in the yard, holding the wrapped dish. From the doorway comes the sound of several people moving quickly in a space too small for them. A pan is set down hard. Someone calls a number, and someone else repeats it. I remember standing outside places where Leo worked, looking for him among windows, unable to imagine what he did between going in and coming out. Jo has a whole other week I have seen in a pile of drawings. Arun has this noise, this warm doorway, and a man who would like him to stay beyond five.

He returns carrying a loaf in a paper bag.

“Bread,” he says. “Not a bribe.”

“Who said it was?”

“Tessa.”

We divide the load. The dish fits under his arm, bread on top. I keep the two empty shopping bags. At the end of the passage he checks his phone, writes a short reply and puts it away.

“Is Jo all right?”

“That was work.”

“Of course.”

He lets us walk a few paces before adding, “She hasn't asked me to report on you.”

“I didn't think she had.”

“Right.”

“Did she?”

He laughs, then shakes his head. I am grateful he does not make more of it. We come out into the market square, where the stalls occupy the same crooked rows I remember. The goods are different in little ways. There are olives in pale buckets and a stand selling covers for phones. The florist has moved to the corner nearest the crossing, or another florist has taken that spot. I stop in front of a bucket of white flowers and cannot remember the name of them.

“For Jo?” Arun asks.

“Just looking.”

I had thought of giving her something. I have arrived empty-handed on the birthday I chose, and the explanations available to me do not alter how empty my hands feel. The flowers are tightly furled, tipped with green. I bend over them. They have almost no smell.

“Peonies,” the woman at the stall says.

“I used to know that.”

“They'll open in the warm.”

I look for a price and find one tucked too far down in the bucket. Before I can ask, Arun has shifted the dish to his other arm, adjusting its weight. I remember the list.

“Perhaps later.”

We buy the things Jo has written down. At the vegetable stall Arun gives me the bag of potatoes while he chooses aubergines. I have to check the list to believe them. Jo used to remove aubergine from anything in which she discovered it, leaving a damp grey heap on the side of her plate.

“These?” I ask.

“They're for the big dish.”

“She eats them now?”

“With considerable enthusiasm.”

He holds one up, looking at its skin. It shines almost black in his hand.

“I used to cook them,” I say.

“Were they good?”

“Apparently not.”

He laughs without taking the bait to reassure me. I choose the tomatoes. Some are so red they look soft, but when I press one gently with my thumb it is firm. I put it in the bag, then choose another. The woman weighing them gives me a cardboard tray to put underneath.

“Stops them bruising.”

The list says lemons, parsley, bread, tins, thick yoghurt. Arun points to the loaf under his arm and draws a line through bread. I can see the pencilled pressure from Jo's hand in the paper. At the bottom she has added something after giving us the bags: WASHING-UP LIQUID. I had hoped it might be a private message, or an extra thing she wanted because I had come.

“No potatoes,” Arun says, seeing what I am still holding.

“You gave me these.”

“That was to hold the bag open.”

I look down. I have adopted the stallholder's paperweight. It is a small net of potatoes with a split at one corner. I take it back, apologising, and the woman laughs.

“You can have them if you want.”

“I think we've got enough to carry.”

It feels good to make her laugh. I almost tell the story of Jo stealing a turnip from a school display, but Arun has got the money out and the woman is weighing the next customer's onions. I keep the story, pleased with myself for having found it again.

At the grocery shop he puts the market bags down just inside the door and reaches for a basket. The dish is becoming awkward now. I hold it while he adds tins and a bottle of washing-up liquid. He checks a message on his phone and adds another carton of yoghurt.

“Jo?”

“She says the one in the fridge is empty.”

“Why is it in the fridge?”

“That is a question for Jo.”

At the till I put my hand into my pocket automatically. There is the envelope, and Jo's pencil, which I have once again failed to give back. No purse. No money. I knew there would be no money, but until this moment I have not needed the knowledge in my hand.

“I've got it,” Arun says.

“I wanted to help.”

“You are holding half a table's worth of crockery.”

He holds his phone over a little black machine. It makes a sound, and the woman hands him a receipt. I remember using cards in shops, but the gesture with the phone seems to have skipped a part I ought to understand. It is already finished before I find the question.

“Does Jo owe you for this?”

“She's sent me money.”

“Already?”

“Before we left.”

“I didn't see.”

“She doesn't make much ceremony of it.”

Outside we put the bags down and rearrange everything. The bottle and tins go with Arun. I take the vegetables. He slides the dish into the larger bag, padded with the tea towel, and puts the bread across the top. There is a moment of pure satisfaction in making the burdens fit. I have the light bag with the knotted handle. The tomatoes sit on their tray and do not crush anything else.

“What was she like?” Arun asks.

I look up, suddenly ready.

“At fifteen,” he adds.

There is too much. I begin with her room, then stop, because I would have to explain the posters and the argument over the carpet before I could get to the part I mean. We start walking. A woman with a pushchair comes towards us, and I move my bag so she can pass.

“She once made a timetable for learning to be less organised,” I say.

“That sounds promising.”

“She thought it would make her interesting. She allowed twenty minutes a day.”

He laughs. I tell him how the timetable had included an interval for doing something spontaneous and how she had become furious when Leo asked what she had planned for it. The memory arrives whole, with the pencil behind her ear and the way she slammed the kitchen door without letting go of the handle. Arun is laughing still when we reach the corner.

“Don't tell her I told you.”

“I'll pretend I knew.”

“She was very serious about it.”

“She sounds like Jo.”

He says it with affection, and I am pleased until I realise he means his Jo, the woman upstairs. He has found a way into the story which does not require me to introduce him to her.

We cross the square again. I look for the white flowers. Their bucket has been moved to the end of the stall, and the woman is wrapping a bunch in paper for somebody else. I imagine setting them on Jo's new table in a jug. I imagine Jo seeing them before she sees me. I don't ask Arun for the money. The thought stays with me, unwanted and persistent, while we carry her dinner up the hill.
