# 9. The seam

The boat shed has a door inside its door. Leo opens the small one and bends to go through. I bend too late and hit my forehead on the frame.

“Careful,” he says.

“I've done that part.”

Beyond him a woman stands beside a boat with a paintbrush in her hand. Her grey hair is pinned up with an orange clip. She looks at my forehead, then at the door.

“We ought to put something soft on that.”

“Foam,” Leo says.

“You said foam in February.”

“I did.”

“It's still a good idea.”

She puts the brush across the tin and gives me her clean hand.

“Bess.”

“Flora.”

“Yes.”

Her fingers are cool. There is a hard lump beside one nail. She lets go and points to a kettle on a shelf.

“Tea if you want it. Water's in the container, not the tap. Tap tastes of the shed.”

I have drunk enough tea to fill a modest aquarium, but I say yes. It gives me something to do while I look around. The place smells of sawdust, paint and cold mud. Strips of sunlight come through high windows and stop on hanging coats. Beneath one of them a pair of boots has flattened sideways. I want to put them upright.

Leo moves a length of timber from the bench for my bag. Then he undoes a bolt and opens the larger door. The river appears at the end of a narrow lawn, dull green beneath the far bank. A low landing stage lies along this side. The boards have darker stripes where water has dried.

“This is it,” he says.

His boat rests on two padded stands. It is longer than it looked in the photograph, with a pale inside and a blue outside. The blue rises slightly higher along one side than the other.

“I masked it badly,” he says, following my hand.

“I wasn't going to say.”

“You were touching it.”

“I can touch a mistake without reporting it.”

He brings a small step so I can look inside without leaning on the edge. There are seats across the boat and a coil of rope beneath one of them. The ends are closed into little compartments. The inside shines unevenly; I can see where the light breaks over a brush mark.

“Where's the bit that leaked?”

He points to a dull strip along a join.

“I thought it was coming through the side. It was following the join from further up. Once I found where, it was straightforward.”

“Finding where took him longer than straightforward,” Bess says.

“I didn't want to put more on without knowing.”

“No.” She draws her brush carefully round a fitting. “You were very firm about that.”

He smiles at her without looking away from the boat. They have had this conversation often enough to know which parts can be left out.

“And now?” I ask.

“Dry. I've had it out twice this week.”

I walk round the end. The boat changes shape as I move, broad from one angle, pointed from another. I had imagined making a boat as a series of large acts involving saws. Most of what I can see looks like the result of waiting for one thing to dry before doing another.

“Did it come in a box?”

“Several flat pieces. And other boxes.”

“That's less romantic.”

“There was quite a lot left to do.”

He shows me the line where two pieces of plywood meet. For the first time today he speaks for a while without watching my face to see whether he should stop. His finger follows a curve. I put mine beside it and feel the smoothness where the join ought to be.

On the wall above the tools hangs a paddle, its blade broad and slightly spoon-shaped.

“Bess's?” I ask.

“Mine's the one with the white tape,” she says. “That one's his replacement.”

“I thought you rowed.”

“I do,” Leo says. “A rowlock came loose on an early try. We used paddles to get back. I leaned mine against the boat when we stopped.”

“Against the outside,” Bess says.

“It seemed to be there quite securely.”

“Until it wasn't.”

He lifts two long oars out of brackets. “These stay in here.”

“Usually.”

“You're not helping my presentation.”

“I thought that was the presentation.”

She grins at me, then returns to her painting. I like her most when she does that: offers something and does not wait to be rewarded.

The leather round one oar's shaft has come loose along a short seam. Leo lays it on the bench and takes a tin from a drawer. There are needles stuck into a cork, a folded piece of sandpaper and a tool shaped like a tiny wooden mushroom.

“I meant to do this yesterday.”

“You were doing other things,” Bess says.

He selects a needle and holds it up to the window. I put my hand out before he asks.

The thread is waxed and stiff. I miss the eye twice, lick the end and miss again. The wax tastes of a candle.

“Don't watch.”

Leo turns his head. I get it through at once and make him look back.

“There.”

“Well done.”

“Don't do the voice.”

“What voice?”

Bess makes a small noise over her painting. I pull a longer length through the needle so it will not slip out and spoil my authority.

He shows me how the old stitches have parted. The leather itself is sound; the holes are a little stretched at one end. I ask to do it. We put the shaft across the bench with the blade supported on a padded stool, and I sit beside the torn seam.

“Old holes,” he says. “Try not to make them bigger.”

One of them has closed. I push the blunt needle against it until Bess sets a pointed tool beside my hand.

“Open it first.”

I ease the point through. The needle follows. Leo holds the oar steady while I pull, and the leather edges draw together. It is an agreeable thing to watch happen. My second stitch lies beside the first, not quite as close as his old ones.

“Jo did the other sort,” Bess says. “On that cushion there. Hers went all the way through before she noticed.”

I look at the cushion on the end of the bench. It has a small square sewn to one side.

“When was Jo here?”

“Last summer,” Leo says. “A couple of times.”

“To help you?”

“To come out.”

I had been saving up the boat to tell her about. I look from the cushion to the open door, trying to put her here, getting into it.

“Did she sit where you sit?”

“Eventually. She wanted to do everything first.”

“That sounds familiar.”

“Does it?”

His hand stays on the shaft. I have crossed the thread where it should lie flat. I draw the needle back through and try again, holding the loose end clear with my little finger.

“She wanted to see if we'd get as far as the bridge,” Bess says. “We didn't. The wind got up. We spent longer eating the sandwiches than going anywhere.”

“What did you have?”

“Cheese,” Leo says.

“And something she brought in a little tub,” Bess says. “Which he put his elbow in.”

Leo looks at the seam. “It was by my elbow.”

“Then she brought you more lunch?”

“On the next trip.”

I laugh and pull the stitch tight too soon. Leo turns the shaft a little so I can get at it. This time I lay the thread along the leather before drawing it through. The crooked loop settles beside the other stitches.

Bess brings over the mugs. Mine has a blue fish with one eye worn away. She puts it well beyond my elbow, looks at the stitches and says the sleeve needs to sit flat on the underside too. We turn the oar together. She presses the leather with her thumb and nods.

“Not too tight at the end.”

I do the last two more slowly. The thread makes a rough sound passing through. Leo shows me how to secure it, and I hold the loose end away while he cuts. There is a short piece left on the bench. I roll it between finger and thumb until it forms a dark little worm.

“The other one's beginning,” Bess says.

Leo fetches it. Three stitches have frayed. He bends close, lifting the broken ends with the needle, and I hold the shaft as he held it for me. His work is slower but more even. At the last hole he makes a small sound and shakes his fingers.

I move the lamp closer. He uses it, lowering his head into the light.

“It's the angle,” he says.

I say nothing. He puts the needle down and flexes his hand. I want to take it and kiss each of his sore fingers, which would make finishing much harder.

Bess closes her tin and starts cleaning the brush. Her sister has an appointment, she tells Leo, and Bess is the transport because her sister has decided the car park is a personal insult.

“What time?” he asks.

“Twenty past four. So I've got to go now if I want to be blamed for anything useful.”

She rinses the brush again, inspecting it before putting it away. Then she asks whether we will be needing the door open much longer. It is a question about a door. Leo tells her he will lock it.

She squeezes my shoulder as she passes. “Mind your head on the way out. It doesn't move.”

We listen to her car start.

“Could I do it?” I ask.

“The stitching's fine.”

“Row.”

He looks up. “Yes. If you wanted.”

I can feel the pull of the thread still in my fingers, the useful resistance. Beyond the open door the water has changed with the wind. Small bright wrinkles move over a darker patch.

“Show me where I'd sit.”

He finds broad pads and wooden chocks. We lower the boat from its stands, one end and then the other, until it rests securely on the floor near the open doorway. He steadies it while I step in. The trouser knees tighten as I lower myself onto the seat. I am absurdly pleased to have changed clothes for something I had not yet decided to do.

He sets the oars in their fittings, blades pointing out through the broad doorway where there is room. I take a handle in each hand. They are smoother than they look.

“Just the position,” he says. “No great sweep in here.”

I draw my hands towards me and hit one against the other.

“A little difference in the height. Look.”

He stands beside the boat and shows me with his empty hands. I try again. One above the other, then away. My wrists want to bend sharply, and he touches one with a finger to show me where to keep it straighter.

“Not all in the arms.”

“I've only got these.”

“You've got your back.”

I sit up. He moves his hand to my shoulder blade, a brief pressure through the white shirt. I make the small movement again and feel the wish for water under it. The boat stays exactly where it is. That becomes, suddenly, intolerable.

“When do you go?”

“Early. Before other people are about.”

“Before seven?”

He looks at the river instead of answering immediately.

“If the weather holds. After it's properly light. Half past five, perhaps.”

“And back?”

“Six at the latest. I'd rather sooner.”

I look down at my hands on the handles. “Can we?”

“Yes. If it's all right in the morning.”

He says it plainly, without a promise to arrange the weather. I let the oars rest and sit for another moment, feeling the narrow seat against the backs of my thighs.

“What if I want to stop?”

“We stop.”

I nod. It is an ordinary arrangement, available to us.

He takes the oars before helping me out. We leave the boat ready on its pads, the equipment together for the morning. I put both mugs by the sink and wipe the damp mark mine has made with a rag. When I lift my bag from the bench, I can still feel the shape of the handles in my palms.
