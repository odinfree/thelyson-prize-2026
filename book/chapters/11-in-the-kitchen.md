# 11. In the kitchen

The smell reaches us on the stairs. Garlic and hot tomato, with something darker underneath. Ellen lifts the cake box above the banister at the turn, and I follow it as though we have come in a procession.

Jo opens the door with a cloth over one shoulder. Her hair is wet at the back. She has changed into a dark blue dress and put an apron over it, tying the knot at the front where she can reach it.

“Don't look in the kitchen yet,” she says.

“What have you done?” Ellen asks.

“Cooked in it.”

The aubergines lie in the big oval dish, their skins wrinkled and their cut sides dark. Beside them a pan of tomato sauce is cooling. The worktop holds two bowls, a sieve, three knives and a mound of parsley in its paper. Jo has done the cooking and postponed most of the clearing up. I understand the choice immediately.

Ellen wants a place for the cake. Jo points towards the sideboard without looking, then looks and remembers the bottles already there. We move the bottles. Ellen unfastens the box to show us what will need protection: pale icing, a scattering of lemon zest, a piece of paper beneath it which is not quite square.

“Lovely,” Jo says.

“Don't put it by the radiator.”

“It's not on.”

“It might get warm.”

Jo moves it another six inches. Ellen is satisfied enough to put her handbag down.

Leo stands in the doorway while we find places for one another. I carry the blue bag into the kitchen and put it beneath a chair in the corner, the handles folded down. The envelope is still zipped into its inner pocket. I push the bag back from the chair legs so no one will step on it.

“Everything?” he asks.

“For now.”

He nods towards the bag. “You've got the old shoes?”

“Yes.”

“I've got a jacket you can use later.”

“Are we walking?”

“If you'd like.”

I look towards Jo, who is lifting the cake out of the box with Ellen's hands ready on either side. For a moment I can have both things: this room becoming a birthday room, and a stretch of pavement beside Leo after the windows have lit up.

“I would,” I say. “Down towards the river.”

“We'll find a way round.”

I follow him into the little hall while Jo and Ellen carry the cake through to the sideboard. The door to the sitting room swings nearly shut behind them.

“Ten?” I ask. “Outside here?”

He looks at me. “If you want to fix a time.”

“I do.”

“All right. I'll come on foot. I'll bring the jacket.”

“Ten.”

I like knowing there is somewhere to go after this, without having to leave yet. He checks the time on his phone as though ten might have moved while we were deciding, then puts it away.

He kisses me at the door. It is a small, ordinary kiss, not hidden and not displayed. Jo calls goodbye to him over Ellen's instructions about the paper beneath the cake. He tells her to have a good evening. Then the door closes and I am staying.

Ellen goes to wash her hands. Jo points me towards the sink.

“Dressing?”

“Show me.”

She takes out the thick yoghurt, a lemon and a jar with a lid that refuses to open. She wraps the cloth round it and tries again. I hold the jar while she turns. It releases so abruptly that the cloth lands in the sink.

“Tahini,” she says.

“I can read.”

“The oil separates. You have to get right down to the bottom.”

I put a spoon into it. The top is liquid and the bottom solid enough to resist. I stir until my wrist begins to ache, moving the spoon along the glass to bring up the paste. Jo clears a space for me without offering to take over.

“This is for the aubergines?”

“And whatever people want to put it on.”

“I used to make yoghurt with garlic.”

“I know. That's where it started.”

She says it while looking for a clean bowl. I nearly ask her to say it again. Instead I scrape the spoon against the jar, watching the thick paste fall.

“How much?”

“Two of those. Then yoghurt. I'll tell you.”

I add the yoghurt. The mixture changes colour, paling around the edges before it becomes even. Jo gives me the lemon. I roll it beneath my hand before cutting it, and she watches the movement.

“You always did that.”

“It gives more juice.”

“I know. I do it.”

We smile at the bowl. I squeeze one half through my fingers, catch two pips and miss a third. It disappears into the dressing. We both look at the spot where it went.

“Someone will find it,” I say.

“Get a spoon.”

I laugh and begin looking. The pip keeps appearing beneath the surface and escaping as I try to lift it. Jo finds it with a second spoon. Between us we trap it against the side.

Ellen comes back with her sleeves rolled up and begins washing the things in the sink. Jo asks her to leave the roasting tray because it needs to soak. Ellen washes it first. The scrape of the brush fills the space beneath our conversation. It is good to have a third person there for a while, capable of being occupied.

I taste the dressing. The sesame is strong and the lemon catches the sore place on the roof of my mouth.

“Garlic?” I ask.

“A little.”

“How little?”

“Less than you think.”

I hold up a clove. She breaks it in half. I could pretend not to notice. Instead I crush the half she gives me and use the edge of the knife to work it with salt.

She is right about the amount. I know when I taste it, though I still miss the old fierce version, which announced itself all through dinner. I give her the spoon. She puts a finger into the dressing, tastes it and adds a splash of water.

“For pouring,” she says.

“I'd have put it on thick.”

“You can have yours thick. Before I do the rest.”

She finds a little bowl and I put some aside. The arrangement is so easy that I feel faintly cheated of an argument I would have said I didn't want.

Ellen finishes the tray and starts drying it. Jo tells her where the clean cloths are. She comes back with a tea towel from a drawer neither of us has used and hangs the wet one over the oven handle. In a short time the kitchen has fewer objects in it and more room for us. Ellen announces that she will go and put the chairs straight. Jo opens her mouth, then closes it.

“Let her,” I say quietly.

“I was going to.”

We listen to a chair being moved.

Jo gives me the parsley. I pick off the coarse ends while she pours the sauce over the aubergines. Steam rises where it meets the warm flesh. She tilts the dish and makes the sauce run into a corner, then wipes the edge with the cloth she has just retrieved from the oven.

“I thought you didn't like those,” I say.

“I didn't like them then.”

“I cooked them until they were soft.”

“I know. That was some of the problem.”

I look at the dish. The cut surfaces are browned, holding their shape beneath the tomato. Mine used to sink into the sauce, pale and yielding. I remember persuading her to try one piece, and another, as if the next mouthful might change the facts of the first.

“You could have said.”

“I did.”

“You could have been more convincing.”

She laughs and I laugh with her, relieved that this particular failure is small enough to serve hot.

“Your tomato sauce, though,” she says. “More or less.”

I ask what less means. She shows me the smoked paprika tin. I smell it, sneeze, and hand it back. She tells me she has left out the sugar. We taste from two spoons. I would still add a little; she wants the sharpness against the dressing. We leave it as it is.

I chop the parsley. The blade is sharper than mine used to be, and at first I keep striking the board too hard. I slow down until the small green pieces gather without jumping. Jo measures rice into a saucepan, then puts some back. She looks towards the two tables.

“Eight,” she says.

“If Patrick comes.”

“He's coming. Nina says he isn't hungry.”

“That usually means—”

“Yes.”

She adds the rice again.

We work without talking for a while. I rinse the board, she finds the lid for the rice. Ellen calls from the other room to ask whether a little jug is for water or flowers, and Jo says either, which fails to satisfy her. I can see my daughter's shoulder moving beneath the apron strap. She reaches past me for a spoon and I move before she has to ask.

“Why thirty?” she says.

The parsley is still stuck to the wet knife. I wipe it carefully into the bowl.

“You used to think thirty was practically the end.”

“I know what I used to think.”

There is no anger in her voice yet, or none I can separate from the effort of asking. She stands by the saucepan with her hand on the handle.

“I thought it would be funny,” I say.

She waits.

“Not only funny. I wanted to see you grown. With a life. I thought by then you'd have—”

“What?”

I look at the little room we have made hot with our cooking. Her dress has a splash of water on the skirt. There is rice on the worktop beside her wrist.

“I don't know. Arrived somewhere.”

“That's a long wait.”

“Yes.”

“For me.”

“Yes.”

She turns on the heat. It is a useful thing for her to do, because I can look at the blue flame instead of her face.

“I wasn't thinking of every year,” I say. “I was thinking of a day. A day when I could come and you'd show me what you were doing, and perhaps you wouldn't need me in quite the same way.”

“I didn't stop needing you because the date wasn't right.”

“No.”

I put the knife down. My hands have nothing in them now. I have waited for this conversation without knowing how much I have depended on her not starting it.

“There were choices,” I say. “I kept changing them. Twenty-one. Twenty-five. Next year, for about an hour. I thought if I came too soon I'd use it up before you had anything new to tell me.”

She looks at me then.

“That sounds dreadful,” I say.

“It does a bit.”

“I don't mean you weren't enough already.”

“I know what you mean.”

I am not sure she does. I am not sure I do. The thing I chose while ill had the clean edges of a date written on paper. I remember being proud that I could still choose something. That is difficult to admit in this kitchen, where she has been dealing with the choice for half her life.

“I wanted a part that hadn't happened,” I say. “I was greedy for it. And frightened I wouldn't get to see anything beyond what I already knew.”

She rests the lid on the saucepan, leaving a small gap.

“I used to think I'd have to get a lot done by today.”

I look at her.

“Then I got annoyed with myself for thinking that. Then I'd start again.”

“Jo—”

“I'm not asking you to do anything about it. You can't.”

“I could say I got it wrong.”

“Would you mean you wish you hadn't come?”

“No.”

“Then don't say that.”

The water starts to make a low sound in the pan. She turns the heat down and checks the lid. I stand beside her, close enough that our sleeves touch, and look at the same unremarkable thing.

“I am glad you're here,” she says. “I can be glad and still not understand why I had to be thirty.”

“I don't think I can make that part satisfactory.”

“No.”

She wipes her hand on the apron, then puts it briefly against my back. I want to turn and hold her. The touch is over before I can make it a request.

From the other room Ellen asks where Jo keeps the matches. Jo shuts her eyes.

“Why?” she calls.

“For the candles.”

“Later.”

“I won't light them now.”

Jo opens her eyes and looks at me. I have started to smile. She tries not to, which is what makes us both laugh. It is a poor answer to the conversation we have been having, and a good sound to hear from her.

She gives me a bowl of leaves from the fridge. I put them into cold water while she checks the aubergines. The parsley is chopped, the dressing made, the rice beginning to cook. I pull one bruised leaf from the water and lay it aside. Jo reaches over me for the salt, and this time I stay where I am while she finds her own way round.
