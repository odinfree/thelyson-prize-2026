# 15. After the plates

The water in the sink is too hot for Jo and not hot enough for me. We arrive at this discovery with both our hands in it.

“Let me put some cold in.”

“It'll cool down.”

“Yes. With cold water.”

She turns the tap and I take my hands out. Steam wets the underside of the cupboard. Someone has stuck a drawing there, where you would only see it while standing at the sink. A horse, or possibly a dog carrying another dog. I look at it rather than ask.

Jo passes me a tea towel. In the other room Patrick is attempting to explain something which Nina already understands. Every time she says that she understands, he starts from an earlier point. I hear my sister laugh. It is not the laugh she uses for me.

The first plate is almost dry by the time Jo gives it to me. I turn it over, wipe the circle underneath and put it on the counter.

“Where?”

“Up there. Left.”

The cupboard holds three different sizes of plate. I have been using the smallest as a dinner plate. It has served perfectly well. I lift the stack and put mine underneath because it is still a little warm, then wonder what difference I think that makes.

“I shouldn't have done the voice,” I say.

Jo works at a dried patch with the edge of the sponge.

“It wasn't just the voice.”

“No.”

The next plate comes out of the water with a strand of something stuck to it. She sees it before I do and takes it back.

“You promised,” she says.

“I know.”

I don't remember the promise. I remember the concert. The folding chairs had been put too close together and Leo's knee touched the man beside him. I remember being proud when she stopped the music, then wanting to hide when everybody looked at her. There is room in my memory for both of those things, and for the conductor's purple shoes. The promise is not there.

“I believe you,” I say. “I don't remember saying it. But I believe you.”

She rubs the sponge round the inside of a glass.

“You said you'd tell Dad the other bit. That I'd sung it. And not make it a thing.”

“I did tell him you sang it.”

“I know.”

There is a place I could go from here. I could explain that when you are a mother, and something your child does strikes you with such pleasure, the wish to tell people is almost physical. I could make the telling another thing she has to understand about me. I pick up the next glass instead.

“I liked them laughing,” I say.

She looks at me then. Not in surprise. Her eyes are tired at the edges.

“I could see.”

“That's the part I can account for.”

The towel squeaks against the glass. I put it down before I polish it any harder.

“I liked them,” I say. “I wanted them to like me. I wanted to have something to bring.”

“You didn't have to bring me when I was ten.”

“No.”

She passes me another plate and we find, for a little while, something to do at the same speed. She washes; I dry. I know the plates now. Their three places in the cupboard. The chipped one goes where the other plates cover the chip. It isn't necessary, but I do it.

Mara comes in carrying a bowl. She holds it out between us.

“Am I interrupting?”

“Washing up,” Jo says.

“A sacred thing. Where does this go?”

“I'll take it.”

“It's not empty. I thought you might want what's left.”

Jo looks into the bowl as if she has forgotten the existence of leftovers. “Yes. Thank you. There's a lid somewhere.”

Mara finds a plate that almost covers it. We all look at the gap.

“Good enough for tonight,” she says.

She leans against the doorframe. She has taken her shoes off; her bare heels look pale against the dark floor. “I loved your story, by the way. About Jo.”

Jo puts a fork into the water.

“I told it badly,” I say.

Mara's face changes. She looks from one of us to the other, caught with her harmless kindness in her hand.

“Oh. Well. I thought—”

“She did get them to start again,” Jo says. “It's all right.”

Mara nods, but stays another moment. Then she says something about getting the last of the plates and leaves with a little unnecessary quickness.

“You needn't do it with everyone,” Jo says.

“I wasn't going to.”

“It'll make them feel they have to say something.”

I turn the tap off. It has been dripping onto the back of her wrist.

From the sitting room comes a crash followed by three voices saying nothing has broken. Jo puts the sponge down.

“That means something has,” she says.

It is a spoon. It has fallen behind the low cabinet and struck a metal tray on the way. Patrick is lying on the floor trying to reach it, while Nina holds his drink. Jo fetches the long wooden ruler from her work bag. She slides it under the cabinet, moves the spoon towards the edge and hands it to me to wash.

“Useful woman,” Patrick says from the floor.

“You could get up,” Nina tells him. “Then there'd be somewhere to stand.”

I am still welcome in this room. It is a relief so large that it embarrasses me. Ellen moves her feet to give me a place beside her, and for a moment I nearly sit. Someone has put another little dish of food on the table. A glass stands over the new chip, hiding it except for the pale edge below.

I hold up the spoon. “Duty.”

Back in the kitchen Jo is searching for containers. Every box has a lid belonging to a different box. We put them in pairs along the counter. I try two that are almost right, pressing harder until she takes one out of my hands.

“It doesn't grow if you press it.”

“Mine used to.”

“That explains the drawer.”

I smile. She fits the correct lid over the box with an ease I resent for half a second, then find pleasurable. There is no reason these two things should keep arriving together, but they do.

The clock on the oven says twenty-one thirty-two.

“Is that right?” I ask.

“About three minutes fast.”

“I said I'd meet your dad at ten.”

Her hands stop on the lid. “Here?”

“Outside.”

“To do what?”

“Walk. Go back to his.”

She turns the box and presses down the corner she has already sealed.

“I thought you'd stay.”

“I've been here all evening.”

“I know. I was here too.”

It comes out harder than either of us expects. We look at the boxes.

“I meant afterwards,” she says. “When they'd gone.”

I hear Patrick laughing through the wall. There is no sound of anybody collecting a coat.

“We didn't say.”

“No.”

“I didn't know.”

“You could stay now you do.”

I lay the tea towel flat on the counter. There is a wet patch shaped like a thumbprint, though it is too big for a thumb. I smooth it with my palm and make it bigger.

“I want to go with him,” I say.

The words are plain and quite ugly in this small warm room. I had hoped to find some that would convey the same wish and give less offence.

“All right.”

“It isn't because of the story.”

“I didn't say it was.”

“I don't want you to think I'm leaving because it's difficult.”

She gives a small breath through her nose. “Mum. You can want to go. I can want you to stay.”

“I do want to stay as well.”

“That doesn't help me much.”

The lid she has fitted lifts at one side. We both see it. She pulls the whole thing off and puts it aside.

I reach for her hand. It is wet, and she lets me hold it awkwardly above the counter. There is a line of soap along the heel of her thumb. I rub it with my own thumb until she says, “Don't. It tickles.”

“Sorry.”

She takes her hand back to rinse it.

“We're going to the boat in the morning,” I say. “If the river's all right. Very early. We should be back at the landing by six. Your dad's usual place.”

“I know where it is.”

“You could come.”

“At six in the morning.”

“Yes.”

She looks towards the voices. Her birthday has not finished. For the first time I have a clear, uncomfortable picture of my invitation arriving inside it: another arrangement, another alarm to set, another set of stairs to come down.

“Or call,” I say. “If you're awake. I'll have his phone near me.”

“I haven't decided.”

“You don't have to decide now.”

This at least I am able to mean. I would like to stay in her kitchen until she decides yes, but I can mean the other thing too.

We finish the washing. Jo leaves the last pan soaking because the brown patch won't come off. I want to scour it for her, to leave something finished, but she needs the counter clear and tells me so. I put the boxes in the fridge where she points. One shelf is lower than the others. I have to turn the last box sideways, and she steadies a bottle while I slide it past.

When I go back into the room, Ellen is looking for her other earring. It is still in her ear. I touch it with one finger, and she slaps my hand, laughing.

“You're going?”

“In a minute.”

She catches my wrist and holds it. Her ring presses against the bone. “Give him my love.”

“You can give it to him yourself.”

“I know. Give it to him anyway.”

I bend and kiss her. She smells faintly of wine and the powder she has worn all day. I leave my cheek beside hers longer than is convenient for either of us, and she pats my arm twice. When I straighten, she is examining the earring again.

The others rise in untidy stages. I say goodbye to them as if I might meet them in the town. I thank Arun for the table, though it is Jo's table and his morning's work I mean. He knows what I mean. He touches my shoulder and says it was good to meet me.

I retrieve the blue bag from beneath the kitchen chair. The green dress is folded over my old shoes, the photograph envelope safe in the little zipped pocket inside. Patrick offers to carry it downstairs. I say I can manage. He looks at it, plainly lighter than what he had imagined, and hands it over without making a thing of it.

Jo comes to the landing. Behind her someone starts washing the pan we have left soaking. We hear a spoon or a knife scraping against it.

“They'll ruin that,” she says.

“Probably.”

She leans against the wall. The light makes a shadow under the little mark beside her eye. I do not ask if it hurts.

“Thank you for having me.”

“It was my birthday.”

“I know.”

She puts her arms around me. The bag is between our legs and I push it aside with my foot. I can feel her breathing through the thin cloth of my shirt. I count two breaths, then lose count because I am trying not to count.

“Happy birthday,” I say into her shoulder.

“You said.”

“I'll say it again.”

“All right.”

We stay that way until her weight changes. I let my arms loosen. She rubs under one eye with the side of her finger, checks the finger and wipes it on her trousers.

I go down carefully, one hand on the rail. At the turn I look up. She is still there, holding the door open with her bare heel. From inside the flat Nina calls a question about the pan.

“Leave it,” Jo calls back. “Just leave it for now.”
