# 6. Places

The table has a bowl on it when we return. It is an ordinary white bowl, empty except for a pencil, but it makes the room look inhabited in a different way. Jo has moved her drawings into the boxes and stacked the boxes beneath the window. The folding table is bare. I can see a pale rectangle where the cutting mat has protected it from the sun.

“Everything?” she asks.

Arun sets down the dish with both hands. “And a towel we have to return before Tessa notices it's gone.”

“She gave you the towel.”

“She did it in stages.”

Jo puts the bread on the table. She gets plates from the kitchen and gives one to me before I can ask what I should do. It has a narrow blue rim, not quite the same blue as the serving dish. I set it beside the new chip, then move it because I don't want anyone to have to sit there. Jo sees me move it. She puts the knife for cutting the bread in the space I've made.

“Do you want cheese?”

“Yes.”

“There's some pickle. If it's still all right.”

“That sounds inviting.”

She brings out a jar, examines its contents and sniffs the lid. Then she passes it to Arun for a second opinion. He sniffs it too and puts it on the table.

“Pickle.”

“Thank you.”

I would have thrown it away after that much consideration. Jo finds a clean spoon and puts some on her plate. Nothing happens to her. I cut a slice of bread and sit beside her, close enough that we have to make an arrangement about our elbows.

Arun is standing in the kitchen, looking at his phone while eating the end of the loaf.

“Sit down,” Jo says.

“I've got to be back for one.”

“It's half past twelve.”

“That's how going somewhere works.”

He sits anyway, pulling the chair out with his foot. Its back is curved and does not match the other two. I look around for more chairs and find a stool under the window and a straight-backed one beside the boxes. There are five seats I can see. I begin to count the people from the telephone call, then stop because I don't know their names.

Jo puts the receipt for the table in the bowl with the pencil. I feel the pencil in my own pocket and take it out.

“I've taken this again.”

“You can keep it.”

“It was behind your ear.”

“I've got another.”

She takes it, because I am holding it out, and puts it with the first. There are two pencils in the bowl now. I watch her reach past it for the bread. The front of her wrist has a faint mark where a watch or bracelet used to sit. There is no ring on either hand. I notice, dislike myself for noticing, and look at the pickle instead.

“How was town?” she asks.

“Still there.”

“Mostly,” Arun says.

“The library's gone,” I tell her, as though she didn't tell me first.

“You should see the new one,” she says. “They've got—”

She stops. I put down my bread.

“What?”

“Big windows. On the river side.”

“We can look as we pass.”

“Yes.”

She takes a bite of bread. I have wanted her to speak without thinking about how long I have, and the first time she does it I make her finish the sentence carefully. I take some pickle. It is sharp and rather good.

Leo rings the bell from downstairs. Arun gets up, grateful perhaps for something to do with the last of his bread, and goes to let him in. We hear their voices on the stairs. Leo comes into the room carrying a small tin and a folded piece of sandpaper. He looks first at me, then at the chair beside me, as though checking where he will fit.

“Lunch?” Jo asks.

“I've had a bit.”

“Have another bit.”

She puts a plate down for him. He leaves the tin beside it and takes the chair Arun has moved nearer the table. For the first time we are all sitting at the thing we brought home. The folded paper under one foot holds. It is sufficiently level that the spoon stays where Jo puts it.

“I looked at those pads,” Leo says. “I thought we'd start with the thin ones.”

“After you've eaten,” Jo says.

He nods, cuts a small piece of cheese and balances it on his bread. A crumb falls onto his jumper. He picks it off and eats it. I used to tell him not to do that in front of people, a distinction which required him to remember that he was married to someone who did not always count as people. I leave him alone.

Arun gets up before the rest of us have finished. He folds the tea towel around the serving dish again and looks at the clock above the kitchen door.

“That can stay,” Jo says. “I'm using it.”

“Habit.”

He uncovers it, puts the towel beside it and collects his jacket. Jo takes her phone out.

“Did I send enough?”

“You sent too much. I'll bring wine.”

“Not instead of eating.”

“I'm working in a restaurant, Jo.”

“That's why I'm saying it.”

He bends to kiss the top of her head. She moves at the same moment to pick up a plate, and he gets her hair in his mouth. They disentangle themselves without embarrassment. He touches my shoulder as he passes.

“See you later.”

I look at Jo. Arun follows my look, then turns towards the door.

“I'm late,” he says, and leaves us with a discretion I wish I hadn't needed.

For a few moments we eat. Leo asks about the mouldings. Jo tells him she sent the wrong file. He asks whether the originals are safe, and she says they are. The conversation travels past me without trying to hide anything. I can understand most of the words and almost none of the decisions behind them.

Jo pushes her plate away.

“Would you like to come for dinner?”

She is looking at me. I hold the spoon over the pickle jar.

“Yes.”

“Six o'clock.”

“Yes.”

“It's not going to be anything very grand.”

“Jo.”

“I'm telling you because you used to take that as a challenge.”

I laugh. She looks down at her plate, and when she looks up again her face has changed a little.

“I'd like you to come.”

“I'd like to.”

The spoon touches the glass. I have been holding it out for so long that a drop of pickle has reached the handle. I wipe it with a piece of bread, then eat the bread. Leo smiles at his plate.

“You too, Dad,” Jo says. “Obviously.”

He takes off his glasses and rubs one eye.

“I'd like the afternoon with Flora. If that's all right. And then I think I'll go home for a bit.”

“You'll need to eat.”

“I can do that.”

“I know.”

He looks at me. “There's a few things I'd like to show you.”

I almost say that Jo has things to show me too. Then I remember the photograph with the boat, still in the envelope against my hip, and how long it took him to finish the story of the paddle.

“All right,” I say.

“I'll have supper at mine. You can call when you're ready afterwards.”

“Will you come back?”

“Yes.”

He says it simply enough that I feel foolish for asking. But I have spent the morning watching people go out of doors I could not follow them through without making a choice. I want to know which of them will open again.

Jo takes a clean page from the pad on the counter. She writes down names, counting on her fingers between them.

“There were six,” she says. “Me, Arun, Nina, Mara, Phil and Ellen.”

“Ellen.”

“She's bringing something.”

“What sort of something?”

“She wouldn't say.”

I laugh before I can help it. My sister has spent her life bringing something whose practical inconvenience is only revealed when she arrives. Jo grins.

“She knows about today?”

“Yes, Mum.”

“Of course she does.”

I picture Ellen as I last saw her, standing by a window, holding a cup by the bowl because the handle had broken. I try to add fifteen years to her. The result is vague, and I turn back to the people at the table.

“Then Patrick makes seven,” Jo says. “If he comes. And you make eight.”

She puts the pencil down and looks around the room. The new table is not growing to accommodate us. I follow her gaze to the cleared folding table by the window.

“Bring that one over.”

“It collapses,” Leo says.

“Only if someone leans on the wrong edge,” Jo says. “I wasn't planning to dispose of it tonight.”

We get up and experiment. The round table moves towards the middle of the room; the folding one comes out from beneath the window. Jo opens its little braces fully and checks them with both thumbs. Together the tables make a shape which looks uncertain until she turns the narrow one a little. The ends are near each other, with room for plates and space to get past. No one has to balance a plate over the gap.

“Two here,” she says, touching the folding top. “Six round this one.”

Leo brings in a chair from her bedroom. It has a cardigan on the back. He hangs the cardigan on the door, then fetches the stool. There are still not enough seats.

“Nina's bringing two folding chairs,” Jo says. “And I've got another in the cupboard. I asked before I bought the table.”

“I didn't say you hadn't.”

“You were counting with your mouth.”

I close it. Then all three of us laugh, briefly and without much sound. Jo stands in the passage between the tables and tests how far she can pull a chair out. It touches the wall. She pushes the round table a few inches away, then tries again. This time the chair comes out and leaves room behind it.

“That works.”

The temporary paper shim has fallen over. Leo opens his tin. Inside are little discs of felt, screws, a stub of candle and an elastic band that has gone brittle. I pick out one of the thinner pads while he feels beneath the short foot. Jo lifts the table just enough for him to fit it. When she lowers it, he presses both hands on the top. No tick.

“Good,” she says.

He passes her the sandpaper. She folds it small and works carefully over the sharp corner of the new chip, keeping a plate underneath for the dust. The pale patch remains. She brushes it with a finger and hands me the paper.

“Feel that.”

I feel the edge. It no longer catches. The broken piece is still folded in the gas bill on the shelf.

“It could go back,” I say.

“Another day.”

She means the repair. I know she means the repair. I fold the paper once more and put it in Leo's tin.

Jo clears the plates while Leo moves the spare chair out of the walkway. I carry the cheese into the kitchen and look at the vegetables we brought. They are a pleasing jumble of shapes. I want to begin, to cut something which will still be there when we sit down tonight.

“Can I help before dinner?”

Jo considers the counter, then me.

“Five?”

“I can come earlier.”

“Five would be useful. I'll do the aubergines before that. You could do the dressing. And we need to set everything out.”

“What dressing?”

“I'll show you.”

I nod. There is a particular pleasure in having been given an hour with a task inside it. I do not have to invent what to say when I arrive. I will be carrying a knife or a lemon, asking where she puts something, and the hour will start that way.

Leo is waiting in the hall. He has put on his coat, then remembered the tin and come back for it. Jo holds the front door for us.

“Happy birthday,” I say again.

“Thank you.”

We kiss, more easily than in the warehouse. Her cheek is warm from washing up. I want to stay long enough to become tired of how warm it is, but Leo is on the stairs and I have said I will go with him. Jo touches the loose thread around my pocket.

“I did that.”

“It was nearly out anyway.”

She gives it a small tug. It holds. Then she lets me go, closing the door only after I have started down the stairs.
