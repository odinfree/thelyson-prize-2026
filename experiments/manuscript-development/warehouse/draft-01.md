Jo is standing on a chair when we find her. She has one foot on the seat and the other on a rail, and is directing a tall man who has put his head through the middle of an upside-down table.

“Not that one. The one by your ear.”

“I can't see my ear.”

“Other side.”

I stop inside the warehouse door. Behind her, a row of mirrors gives me several small views of her back. Her hair is short enough to show the top of her neck. There's a pale patch there where the hair has been cut more recently than the rest.

“Jo.”

She turns too quickly. The chair tips and the man reaches out of the table to catch it.

“Jesus,” he says.

“Sorry. Sorry.”

She is saying it to him. She gets down, puts the chair straight and stands with both hands on its back. She looks at Leo first.

I have thought of her face so often that the real one takes a moment to displace it. The nose is right. The mouth, pressed into itself, is right. There is more face between them than I remember, though I don't suppose there can be. She has a little red mark beside one eye.

“You've hurt yourself.”

I hear my first words to her and would like to put them back.

“It's a spot.”

“Oh.”

“Hello, Mum.”

Her hands leave the chair. I reach her before I decide to move. We come together hard enough for her chin to hit my cheek, and then she puts her chin over my shoulder. She is taller than I am. Her jacket smells of a damp place and of the inside of a pencil case. I hold the back of it with my hands spread wide.

“Sorry,” she says again, into my hair.

“What for?”

“Your face.”

“It's a spot.”

She makes a noise against my ear. I squeeze her. There is a seam under my thumb and I rub it, because stopping my hand seems likely to stop everything.

Then she moves and I let go. A zip on her jacket has caught in the yellow stitching of my pocket. We remain joined while she picks it loose.

“Hold still.”

“I am.”

“You're following me.”

I plant my feet. She frees us.

The tall man is still inside the table. He has folded his arms on its underside and is looking determinedly at a cupboard across the aisle.

“This is Arun,” Jo says. “Arun, this is my mum.”

“Hello.” He brings a hand out between two rails. “I'd get up, but I think I might be wearing it.”

I shake his hand. His palm has a small washer stuck to it.

“You've lost something.”

He looks at his hand, then at the floor, already too late.

“Oh, good,” Jo says. “That's only the part which stops dinner landing on our knees.”

Leo puts the bag of clothes on a chest of drawers and drops onto one knee. He finds the washer beside a wheel from some other piece of furniture.

“Here. Have you got something to put these in?”

Jo produces a paper sandwich bag from her jacket. There is a grease stain across the bottom.

“Breakfast?” I ask.

“Second breakfast. Arun brought it.”

I look at Arun with an affection he has done little to earn and which may not be very comfortable for him. He crawls out, brushing dust from his knees.

“She's been here since nine,” he says. “I've learned more about beetles than I meant to.”

“It's old damage,” Jo says. “Probably.”

“How can you tell?”

“The holes are dark inside. No fresh dust underneath. It's a good sign, not a promise.”

“So there could be something alive in it.”

“Probably more in your shoe.”

I look down.

“She means spiders,” Arun says.

“I don't need the classification.”

Jo grins. I keep looking at the gap beside my sock, which I hadn't minded until now.

The table lies across a pair of trestles. Its four legs point up like a beast waiting to have its feet examined. One is already loose. I put my hand around it. The wood is cold, then less cold.

“Oak,” Jo says.

“Is that good?”

“For a table.”

“I'm catching up.”

She looks at me, and I cannot tell whether I've spoiled something. Then she lifts a curl of fluff off my sleeve.

“You haven't missed a change in wood.”

Leo checks the bolts without speaking. He turns the loose leg, feeling the resistance, and puts it back into place.

A man in a purple fleece arrives carrying a mug with no handle. “Decided?”

“Nearly,” Jo says. “What's this?”

She points beneath the edge. There is a split along a short section of wood, dark in the middle. The man bends, not quite far enough to see it.

“Age. They all have a bit of that.”

“It moves.”

She presses with her thumb. I see the split open a little. Arun bends to look as well, and the man finally puts his mug down.

“Doesn't affect the table.”

“It affects the price.”

The man looks from her to Leo. Leo has his glasses halfway down his nose and is studying a bolt as though nobody has spoken.

“Eighty-five's very fair,” the man says.

“And fifteen to deliver.”

“You wouldn't get it in your car.”

“Which is why we're discussing delivery.”

I want to step in. Fifteen pounds is a small amount to be stopped by. I have no money, but this seems a technical objection: Leo has money, and I know how to ask him with a look.

Jo takes out her tape measure instead.

“Would you hold that?”

She gives the end to me. I hook it over the far edge, and she draws the yellow strip towards her. It sags between us.

“Pull a little.”

I pull too much. The hook comes off and the tape snaps back across the wood.

“Sorry.”

“Again.”

We do it again. This time I hold the hook itself. My knuckle presses into the oak.

“One hundred and eighteen,” she says. “That's all right.”

“Your dad says the door's seventy-four.”

“Centimetres. We stand the top on its edge.”

“I know that.”

I didn't, until she said it. I had pictured us rolling the whole thing up the stairs like an enormous wheel.

“The turn is the awkward bit,” Leo says. “Might need to lift it above the radiator.”

Arun rubs the back of his neck. “Excellent. A reason to have arms.”

Jo lets the tape return slowly through her fingers. She holds the metal hook between her teeth while she writes something on the back of her hand.

“Seventy-five,” she says to the seller, after removing it. “And the fifteen.”

“Eighty.”

“Including delivery?”

He laughs. She waits. Somewhere deeper in the warehouse a radio loses its signal, recovers a woman's voice and loses it again.

“Eighty, and ten to take it round. We're passing there anyway.”

“Ninety altogether?”

“That's what I said.”

“That's what I'm checking.”

She brings out her phone. I have an absurd urge to remind her not to spend all her birthday money. Her thirtieth birthday money. I bite the inside of my cheek.

The seller leads her to a desk. While he finds his little machine, she turns back.

“Mum, could you keep hold of that bag? With the washers. Please don't put anything else in it.”

I take the sandwich bag from Leo. Inside are two bolts, one washer and a few crumbs. I separate the crumbs with my finger.

“What does she do?” I ask him quietly.

He looks towards the desk.

“For work. I know she hasn't become a table inspector.”

“Conservation. Buildings. She works for a small practice.”

“Like churches?”

“Sometimes.”

“She used to put chewing gum under her school desk.”

“I don't think she does that now.”

He has found the right spanner in a tray beneath the trestle. He offers it to me handle first.

“Here. Hold it flat against the nut. Turn towards you.”

Arun holds the leg. I turn. Nothing happens. I turn harder and the spanner slips, making a noise loud enough for Jo to look round.

“It's all right,” I tell her.

She looks at the spanner rather than at me, then returns to paying.

Leo moves my thumb. “Keep it here. Now.”

The nut gives. I am prepared for a struggle and nearly hit him in the jaw.

“That enthusiastic?” Arun says.

“I've been wanting to do that for years,” I say, and Leo laughs before I can explain which part I mean.

We remove the leg. It is heavier than I expect. The square end is pale where it has been hidden, with a pencil figure written across it. Two.

“Mark where it goes,” Leo says.

“Somebody has.”

“Then check the others.”

I find a three and a four. On the last leg the figure has rubbed away. Jo returns with a receipt and takes a pencil out of her hair. I hadn't noticed it there. It was hidden almost entirely along the short hair behind her ear.

“Do you always carry that?”

“Usually when I can't find it.”

She draws a one on the leg, then a matching one on the underside. Her handwriting is nothing like the writing in her schoolbooks. She gives me the pencil, and I finish darkening the figure while she folds the receipt.

“Bought it,” she says.

“Happy birthday,” Arun says.

“I'd better like it.”

We help the seller turn the top. In the light its surface is an uneven honey colour. There is a darker ring near one side, larger than a cup, and a shallow groove that wanders towards the middle. Jo runs her fingers along it.

“You can sand that out,” I say.

“I'd rather eat at it.”

She leans both forearms on the top. Even on the trestles it holds her weight without a wobble. Her expression changes, a small satisfaction I would have missed if I had been watching for something bigger.

“Come and help,” she says to me. “At the flat. If you're not sick of it already.”

“I'm not.”

“Arun's staying for the heavy part. You could tell us when we're about to break something.”

“A responsible position.”

“You've had practice.”

She takes the sandwich bag out of my hand and counts everything. Then she gives it back. Four bolts now, four washers, no crumbs.

The seller opens the loading door. Daylight shows a fine layer of dust on all our sleeves. Leo collects the blue bag from the chest of drawers, checks the pockets of his coat for the keys and looks at me.

“Ready?”

“One minute.”

I rub out a smudge beside the number I've drawn beneath the table. It doesn't need to be done. Jo has gone to the loading door with Arun, and I have to hurry after them, holding the small bag upright so none of the pieces can fall out.
