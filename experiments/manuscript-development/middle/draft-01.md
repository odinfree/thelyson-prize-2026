The boat shed has a door inside its door. Leo opens the small one and bends to go through. I bend too late and hit my forehead on the frame.

“Careful,” he says.

“I've done that part.”

Beyond him a woman is standing beside a boat with a paintbrush in her hand. Her grey hair is pinned up with an orange clip. She looks at my forehead, then at the door.

“We ought to put something soft on that.”

“Foam,” Leo says.

“You said foam in February.”

“I did.”

“It's still a good idea.”

She puts the brush across the open paint tin and gives me her clean hand.

“Bess.”

“Flora.”

“Yes.”

She doesn't say anything after it. Her fingers are cool and there is a hard lump beside one nail. She lets go and points to a kettle on a shelf.

“Tea if you want it. Water's in the container, not the tap. Tap tastes of the shed.”

The place smells of sawdust, paint and cold mud. Strips of sunlight come through the high windows and stop on a row of hanging coats. Beneath one of them a pair of boots has flattened sideways. I find myself wanting to put them upright.

“This is it,” Leo says.

His boat is on two padded stands. It is longer than it looked in the photograph and much narrower, with a pale inside and a blue outside. Along one side the blue rises slightly higher than on the other.

“I masked it badly,” he says, following my hand.

“I wasn't going to say.”

“You were touching it.”

“I can touch a mistake without reporting it.”

He turns a bucket over so I can see inside without leaning my weight on the edge. The wood is clean, the little compartments in the ends bare. No fishing things, no complicated equipment. A coil of rope sits under a seat.

“Where's the bit that leaked?”

He points. I lean forward. There is a dull patch which would have meant nothing if he hadn't shown me.

“It hasn't since.”

“How often do you go?”

“Twice last week.”

I try to arrange him in the boat. I have seen the photograph but it is easier to imagine the Leo I knew, who would have spent an afternoon looking for his gloves while the afternoon went away.

He takes two oars from brackets on the wall. One has a brown leather sleeve coming loose around its shaft. The stitching has broken along a short section.

“I meant to do this yesterday.”

“You were doing other things,” Bess says.

She has resumed painting. Whatever the other things were, she does not list them.

Leo rests the oar on a bench and brings a tin of small tools out of a drawer. There are needles in a cork, a folded piece of sandpaper and several things I cannot name. He chooses a needle and holds it up to the window.

“Would you?”

I put out my hand. He gives me the needle and a length of waxy thread.

The eye isn't very small. That ought to make it easy. I miss twice, lick the end of the thread and miss again. The wax tastes of a church candle.

“Don't watch.”

Leo turns his head obediently. I get the thread through at once and make him look back.

“There.”

“Well done.”

“Don't do the voice.”

“What voice?”

Bess makes a little noise over her painting, not quite a laugh. I hand him the needle.

He examines the old holes along the edge of the leather. “It needs a few stitches. The sleeve's still sound.”

“Let me.”

“You don't have to.”

“I'm very good at making holes in things. I've spent the morning on furniture.”

He gives me the needle again. I sit at the bench, putting the oar across my lap. It is badly balanced that way: the blade swings out and nearly sweeps a jar off the next shelf. Leo moves the jar and finds something to support the blade.

“Through the old holes,” he says. “Don't pull them longer.”

The first hole is easy to find. The one opposite has closed. I push the blunt needle at it until Bess puts a little pointed tool beside my hand.

“Open it with that. Save yourself swearing.”

“I wasn't.”

“Saving it up, were you?”

I ease the hole open. The needle follows, drawing the leather edges closer. Leo holds the oar steady while I make the next stitch. His hand rests a few inches from mine. I put the needle down for a moment and turn his wrist so the sleeve of his jumper doesn't catch it.

“That darn,” I say. “Who did it?”

“I did.”

“You?”

“It was a hole.”

“I noticed the wrong blue.”

“Bess noticed it first.”

“I said I had blue wool,” she says. “I never said it was the right blue.”

“Do you live near each other?”

“Opposite ends of the lane,” Leo says. “Mine's the little cottage by the allotments.”

I have put the needle through the same hole twice. There is a loop where the thread should lie flat.

“Your cottage?”

“Yes.”

“What happened to our house?”

He takes his hand off the oar. I have to catch it before it turns.

“I sold it.”

Bess lays down the brush. She picks up the kettle, shakes it and takes it to the water container. There is a lot of room in the shed for her to move, but I feel her passing close behind me.

“Who to?”

“A family. I didn't know them.”

“You sold it to strangers.”

“They generally are.”

I look at him. He looks at the loop in my stitching.

“Sorry. That sounded—”

“Yes.”

I take the needle backwards through the leather. The loop tightens into a knot. I pull harder, and the whole oar moves.

“Flora.”

“I'm undoing it.”

“I can see.”

He holds the shaft again. I work the knot open with my fingernail. The thread has made a red line across my finger.

“I thought we'd go there,” I say.

“I know.”

“You could have told me.”

“I was trying to find when.”

“Before now.”

“Yes.”

The water takes a long time to reach the kettle's minimum line. Bess stops pouring twice to see how far it has got.

“Did you want tea?” she asks.

“Yes, please,” I say, more loudly than I need to.

Leo says, “I live on my own. There. At the cottage.”

He has answered something I haven't asked. I am angry with him for thinking of it and relieved enough to be angry about that as well.

“Jo's old room,” I say.

“She'd moved out.”

“The wall with the pencil marks.”

“Yes.”

“Did you paint over them?”

“No.”

I want to ask what the new people have done. He cannot know. I find the right hole and pass the needle through. The next stitch sits beside it, small and crooked. Leo keeps holding the oar.

Bess puts a mug on the bench. There is a blue fish on it with one eye worn away. She goes back to her boat.

“It's got a garden,” Leo says.

“Most cottages do.”

“A small one. I wanted you to see it.”

I drink the tea too soon. It isn't as hot as the tea at Rose's, but the sore patch on my mouth finds it.

“I said I'd go back to Jo.”

“We will.”

“I don't know what time it is.”

He shows me his watch without taking his hand off the wood. There is time. I resent the watch for making this so plain.

I finish the line of stitching. Leo shows me where to secure the end, and Bess comes to inspect it before I cut the thread.

“That'll sit all right,” she says. “Turn it.”

We turn the oar. She feels the other side of the leather, checks the old stitches with a nail and lets go.

“Other one's starting to fray, Leo. Look at that before you take them.”

He puts it beside the repaired one. They make a handsome pair, even with my crooked stitches.

Bess washes her brush. She tells Leo where she has put the lid from the paint tin, though he is looking at it.

“My sister's making lunch. If I'm late she'll feed mine to the dog in front of me.”

“How is the dog?” he asks.

“Fat.”

She wipes her hands on a rag, squeezes my shoulder as she passes and bends carefully through the little door.

We listen to her car start. I put both hands around my mug. Leo inspects the stitching on the second oar.

“Do you want me to do that one?”

“I'll see how much it needs first.”

He bends his head close. In the window light I can see the little scrape where his glasses have rubbed behind his ear. He lifts one stitch with the point of the needle, then draws out a broken end.

“Three, I think.”

I put my tea down and hold the oar as he held it for me. He is slower than I was, but his stitches come out even. On the last one he gives a little grunt and shakes his fingers. I move the lamp closer without asking him whether he needs it. He uses it, lowering his head into the light.

“Would I be able to do it?” I ask.

“The sewing?”

“Row.”

He looks up. “A bit. If you wanted.”

“Does that mean yes?”

He puts his mug down.

We carry the boat off its stands and rest it on two broad pads on the floor. He holds it while I step in, then helps me sit on the narrow rowing seat. I have to draw up my skirt. My knees look surprisingly large in the pale little hull.

He gives me the repaired oar to hold across my thighs.

“Not sweeping it about in here,” he says. “Just feel where your hands go.”

I wrap my fingers round the handle. It has been rubbed smooth by use. I pull towards myself, elbows out, and bump my stomach.

“Other way.” He stands behind me, moving my hands apart. “You don't need so much.”

I try again, more slowly. My shoulders move, then my back. I can feel the effort wanting something to push against. Outside, a bird makes a loud repeated noise from the gutter. I imagine the boat slipping away from that sound.

“When do you go?”

“Early, usually. It's quiet.”

“Before seven?”

He is still holding one of my hands. He lets it move to the end of the handle.

“We'd need to watch the time.”

“We've been watching it all day.”

“And the river.”

“If it's all right.”

“If it's all right.”

I put the oar down carefully. My legs are stiff when he helps me out, and I am furious with them. Then I discover I have left a damp ring from my mug on the bench. I wipe it with the hem of my dress before Leo sees.
